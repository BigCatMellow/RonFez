# Part 4 — XM, 2005–2007: Black Earl, East Side Dave, Paltalk, and the expanding universe

## Summary

Early XM turned the machinery built at WNEW and WJFK loose. Satellite gave Ron and Fez national reach, fewer content restrictions, and—crucially—more time. Conversations could run until they exhausted themselves. That was almost perfectly suited to Ron's style.

At nearly the same moment, Fez suffered his first heart attack, Black Earl became increasingly valuable as an unintentional character, and East Side Dave joined the staff. By 2007 the nominal hierarchy—Ron and Fez as hosts, everyone else as support—no longer fully described what listeners were hearing.

## Move to XM

Ron and Fez began regular XM broadcasts on **September 12, 2005**, on High Voltage 202.

The key change was not simply the ability to swear. Former executive producer Earl Douglas later emphasized the freedom from rigid terrestrial-radio clocks. If something became interesting, Ron could keep following it.

This was ideal for the show's increasingly conversational structure:

`question → strange answer → follow-up → contradiction → explanation → worse contradiction → forty-five minutes vanish`

## Black Earl

Earl Douglas had been with Ron and Fez since WNEW and became executive producer during the satellite era.

Reducing Earl to “the guy who lies” misses why he worked so well. The actual comedy mechanism was usually **explanation under pressure**.

Ron would identify one small inconsistency in something Earl said. Earl would try to clarify it. The clarification would generate another contradiction. Ron would continue asking calm, reasonable questions until an ordinary production mistake sounded like a conspiracy.

This became a recurring R&F genre.

### Why Earl mattered structurally

Earl could interact productively with almost everyone:

- Ron interrogated him;
- Fez judged him;
- Dave attacked him;
- fans wrote about him;
- Board Gossip amplified those discussions;
- other staff used him as a source of grievances.

He became a **bridge character** across multiple eras and social groups.

## Fez's first major cardiac crisis

On **November 9, 2005**, shortly after the XM move, Fez suffered a heart attack. He initially did not fully recognize what had happened, later sought medical care, underwent angioplasty, and returned to the show later that month.

This is a major chronological marker but should not be simplified into a single-cause story. Fez's later broadcasting difficulties involved multiple documented factors, including cardiac illness, depression, anxiety, performance anxiety, and sexuality-related identity conflict.

The important point is that a serious physical-health crisis began at the same time the show was expanding creatively and socially.

## East Side Dave arrives

East Side Dave McDonald had already orbited the show before formal employment. His later retrospective dates his joining the staff to **December 2005**.

Dave initially imagined himself contributing more conventional scripted comedy. An old notebook he later discussed with Ron contained sketches and assigned characters. What Ron actually found more useful was **Dave himself**.

Dave was:

- loud;
- competitive;
- thin-skinned;
- shameless;
- obsessed with wrestling, sports, movies, and pop culture;
- willing to turn his personal life into material;
- constantly creating characters and songs;
- willing to be humiliated if the humiliation produced radio.

Where Earl often tried to escape Ron's trap, Dave frequently jumped into it and made it larger.

## Dave versus Earl

Dave and Earl became one of the classic satellite pairings because their personalities were nearly opposites.

| Earl | Dave |
|---|---|
| avoids conflict | searches for conflict |
| reluctant | overeager |
| defensive | prosecutorial |
| tries to disappear | wants airtime |
| understated | theatrical |
| explains | accuses |

Ron could often create an entire segment with little more than:

**“Dave says Earl didn't do his job.”**

The workplace friction appears to have had a genuine basis, but both men learned to perform and exaggerate it for radio. This is a recurring R&F pattern: **real conflict becomes structured entertainment**.

## Board Gossip

East Side Dave turned the fan ecosystem itself into an explicit segment through **Board Gossip**.

The basic loop:

1. Fans discuss the show online.
2. Dave reads the discussion.
3. Dave reports the discussion on the show.
4. Ron comments on Dave's report.
5. Fans post about Ron discussing their posts.
6. Dave reports that reaction later.

This is one of the clearest examples of the secondary universe becoming part of the production pipeline.

It also made the show more insular. If a listener did not know the boards or the people being discussed, entire segments could feel incomprehensible.

## Paltalk

The **Ron and Fez Big ASS Room** on Paltalk became a major live fan environment. Its surviving listing described it as created “by the fans for the fans.”

Unlike a message board, Paltalk provided synchronous interaction. Fans could listen to the show while reacting together in real time, sometimes with webcam access and occasional visibility into what happened around the studio after the official broadcast.

This made the fan community feel less like a website and more like a **parallel studio/audience room**.

## The lifestyle at peak density

A heavily involved listener could:

- listen to XM at work;
- read RonFez.net simultaneously;
- keep Paltalk open;
- recognize screen names being discussed on-air;
- attend a bar or event later;
- see staff or other listeners there;
- watch something happen socially;
- see photos and arguments about it online;
- hear Ron investigate it the next day.

For some listeners, this was a genuine social scene centered around the show.

## Dave and private life as content

Dave's relationship and eventual wedding became part of the R&F universe. His 2007 wedding to Casey at the Hard Rock Cafe in Times Square was documented in RonFez.net fan photography and became radio/event continuity.

This is a major example of how the show collapsed the boundary between work and private life:

`producer's relationship → show storyline → live event → fan gathering → archive → future radio continuity`

It also illustrates one of the risks of the R&F incentive structure: if personal disaster produces compelling radio, a performer as uninhibited as Dave has little incentive to keep private life private.

## Fez and Dave move in opposite directions

This is one of the central tensions of 2006–2007.

- Dave becomes increasingly willing to expose himself.
- Fez becomes increasingly guarded and inconsistent.
- Dave wants the microphone.
- Fez sometimes struggles to use it.
- Dave treats humiliation as fuel.
- Fez's private difficulties increasingly make performance painful.

Ron now has two very different human engines available to him.

## Free FM dual-show period

Beginning in December 2006, Ron and Fez also returned to New York terrestrial radio on 92.3 Free FM while continuing XM.

For several months they effectively did two separate daily programs, one satellite and one FCC-compliant terrestrial show.

This created a revealing contrast:

- XM could assume deep continuity and move slowly through long conversations;
- terrestrial radio needed to work for more casual listeners and tighter restrictions.

Outsiders sometimes reacted with bafflement to hearing lengthy discussion of East Side Dave's relationship or internal staff issues. Longtime listeners heard serialized continuity; newcomers heard office gossip.

## 2007 ensemble density

By 2007, one broadcast could plausibly include combinations of:

- Ron;
- Fez;
- Earl;
- Dave;
- Pepper Hicks / Chris Stanley;
- Paul O;
- Bronx Johnny;
- Crazy Jen;
- Sheepy;
- Mafia Life Chris;
- Big A;
- Stalker Patti;
- celebrity guests;
- old O&A personalities;
- Paltalk and board references.

That is why random clips from this era can feel like they are missing an invisible second show.

The invisible show was everything happening in the social world between broadcasts.

## Second cardiac episode

In May 2007, Fez had another serious cardiac episode requiring another stent.

Again, the show surrounding him was getting larger, louder, and more demanding at the exact moment his physical and emotional ability to sustain that environment was becoming less certain.

## O&A suspension and channel role

When Opie & Anthony were suspended from XM in 2007, R&F temporarily carried even more weight within the channel's programming ecosystem. This highlighted how far the show had come from being the smaller companion program in the WNEW/O&A world.

## Core conclusion

Early XM is probably the fullest expression of the original R&F social model:

`radio + boards + Paltalk + live events + staff as characters + fans as characters + O&A crossover + real lives unfolding publicly`

XM did more than remove censorship.

It removed **boundaries**: time, geography, professional roles, and increasingly private life.

That produced extraordinary radio and created conditions that would become increasingly difficult for the people inside it.
