# Part 2 — WNEW, 2000–2003: when the audience entered the show

## Summary

WNEW is where *Ron & Fez* became more than two people doing a radio program. New York radio, the Opie & Anthony ecosystem, unusually eccentric staff and callers, RonFez.net, live appearances, and early internet culture combined to create a **persistent universe** that continued after the microphones shut off.

By the end of the WNEW era, a listener could become known on the message board, call the show, attend an event, get mocked on-air, have the board discuss that mockery, and then call back about the board discussing the show discussing them.

That recursive relationship became one of the defining mechanisms of *Ron & Fez*.

## Arrival at WNEW

*Ron and Fez Dot Com* debuted on WNEW-FM in New York on **February 21, 2000**, initially in a late-night slot. WNEW was in the middle of a transition from legendary rock station to FM talk outlet, with Opie & Anthony becoming the station's major cultural and ratings center.

The original “Dot Com” idea involved Ron and Fez browsing the internet and talking about odd material they found online. That premise did not remain central for long, but the internet became important in a much more consequential way: it became the place where the audience organized itself.

## WNEW versus O&A

Ron & Fez and Opie & Anthony occupied the same ecosystem but had noticeably different comic instincts.

A useful simplification:

| O&A often generated comedy by… | Ron & Fez increasingly generated comedy by… |
|---|---|
| creating an event | noticing an event |
| setting up a stunt | finding a strange person |
| confrontation | conversation and interrogation |
| escalating outward | drilling inward |
| using comedians as major engines | turning staff/callers/friends into recurring characters |

This is an oversimplification, but it helps explain why R&F evolved toward relationship-driven continuity.

## Staff become serialized characters

The WNEW period contains early examples of a pattern that would later dominate the show: employees become more interesting than their nominal jobs.

### Billy Staples

Billy Staples, real name William Hine, worked as a phone screener but became memorable because his actual life became serialized content.

Recurring material included:

- drinking;
- injuries;
- acting ambitions;
- family stories;
- explanations that generated more questions than answers;
- situations such as paintball incidents and later “Slam Time” lore.

The important structural lesson is that Billy did not need to perform a fictional character. **Billy himself became the character.**

### Al Dukes

Al Dukes entered as a producer but became useful because his discomfort, routines, workplace behavior, and resistance to being turned into material were themselves funny.

### Hawk and other staff

Other staff, including Hawk and Giant Brian Carothers, became part of a widening ensemble in which the boundary between producer and performer was weak.

## Fictional callers and real callers coexist

A later forensic correction is important here: not every “caller” remembered from early R&F was a real eccentric listener.

The show also used **constructed recurring callers**:

- **Iris** — voiced by Ron Bennington;
- **Kenny Allen** — voiced by Ron's son Miles Bennington;
- **Jan Brady** — performed by Fez;
- **André the Giant** — performed by Fez;
- **Mr. Rogers** — performed by Giant Brian Carothers.

These fictional callers existed alongside genuine callers and recurring real-world personalities. Unless a listener knew the continuity, the broadcast did not necessarily stop to announce which category they were hearing.

That is an early version of the work/shoot ambiguity that later defined the show.

## The show develops memory

Traditional radio largely disappears after broadcast. *Ron & Fez* increasingly developed **long-term continuity**.

The show remembered:

- what Billy did months earlier;
- which callers had specific habits;
- old grudges;
- previous bits;
- staff mistakes;
- relationships among recurring people.

The audience remembered too.

That memory eventually became externalized in **RonFez.net**.

## RonFez.net

By 2001, RonFez.net had an active forum community and was developing into far more than a show-discussion board.

The surviving archive includes:

- audio clips;
- early soundboards;
- photographs;
- fan-created media;
- event documentation;
- character material;
- ordinary social discussions unrelated to that day's show.

This distinction is crucial. People did not merely visit the site to discuss *Ron & Fez*. They used it to talk to **other Ron & Fez people about life**.

That is why “fan site” undersells it.

## The Big ASS identity

The show created the **All Secret Society** and the **Big ASS Card**.

Cardholders had membership numbers, could identify themselves when calling, and participated in an intentionally ridiculous ritual of belonging. There were occasional sponsor benefits and on-air acknowledgment.

The card mattered less as a commercial perk than as a status marker:

**I belong to this world.**

Decades later, fans still remembered and referenced Big ASS numbers during Fez's retirement and memorial events.

## Feedback loop

By this era, the show began developing a loop that would later become explicit through Board Gossip:

`SHOW → LISTENERS → MESSAGE BOARD → FRIENDSHIPS/FEUDS → CALLERS/EVENTS → SHOW DISCUSSES THEM → BOARD REACTS → SHOW DISCUSSES THE REACTION`

The key point is that the audience stopped being outside the content pipeline.

It became a **source of content**.

## September 11, 2001

Ron and Fez were on the air in New York on the evening of September 11. The show necessarily became something unlike its normal comedy format: listeners called with information, fear, reactions, and attempts to understand what had happened.

The RonFez.net archive later preserved the **complete September 11 broadcast** as an explicit exception to its general practice of not distributing full shows.

This matters both historically and socially. The same network usually used for absurd comedy suddenly became a communications and emotional-support network during a catastrophe.

## New York Forever and physical community

After September 11, the show participated in **New York Forever**-style gatherings and bar crawls intended to bring listeners back into lower Manhattan businesses.

These events helped turn usernames into real people:

- board members met;
- listeners met hosts and staff;
- friendships and rivalries moved offline;
- Monday's show could discuss what happened over the weekend.

The online/offline boundary was already dissolving.

## Audience participation becomes production

Fans were not merely archiving the show. They were creating things:

- soundboards;
- songs;
- edited audio;
- photos;
- animations;
- recurring jokes.

These creations could then migrate back onto the program.

The production pipeline therefore became:

`show → fan reaction → fan creation → board circulation → show reuse`

This is recognizable today as participatory creator culture, but R&F was doing it in an early-2000s radio context.

## Why old WNEW audio can feel impenetrable

By this point, names began carrying compressed history.

A new listener heard a name.

An old listener heard:

- previous calls;
- board history;
- staff relationships;
- event history;
- running jokes;
- private context that had leaked into radio.

This created unusually strong loyalty but terrible onboarding.

The more you listened, the richer the show became. The less context you had, the more it sounded like walking into someone else's family reunion.

## Collapse of WNEW talk

The 2002 **Sex for Sam** scandal involving Opie & Anthony created major regulatory and corporate fallout. O&A were fired and the WNEW talk format destabilized. By early 2003 the station abandoned that experiment.

Ron & Fez survived through WJFK in Washington.

The important historical result is that the audience did not dissolve when WNEW collapsed.

RonFez.net, callers, jokes, relationships, and archives already existed independently of the station.

**The community had become portable.**
