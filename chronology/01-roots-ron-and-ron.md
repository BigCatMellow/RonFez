# Part 1 — Before *Ron & Fez*: Ron & Ron, Todd Hillier, and the show's original DNA

## Summary

The *Ron & Fez* story begins before the title existed. Ron Bennington's earlier work with Ron Diaz on *The Ron & Ron Show* established several habits that would become central later: ensemble comedy, recurring personalities, live events, willingness to turn staff and acquaintances into material, and the idea that a radio show could create a social world larger than the hours it was on the air.

## Ron Bennington before Fez

Ron Bennington worked as a stand-up comedian and ran a comedy club in Clearwater, Florida before becoming a major radio personality. He partnered with Tampa broadcaster Ron Diaz in 1987 on WYNF/95YNF. The *Ron & Ron Show* became a successful Florida morning program and eventually syndicated beyond Tampa.

Contemporary and retrospective accounts describe an unusually permissive production culture. The show used live events, touring comedy, recurring people around the program, and large listener gatherings. Diaz later recalled some public events drawing roughly 15,000 people.

This matters because *Ron & Fez* did not invent its community instinct from nothing. A basic model was already present:

`hosts → producers → recurring people → live events → audience → more material`

## Todd Hillier becomes Fez Whatley

Todd Hillier joined the *Ron & Ron* world around 1990 as a producer/on-air contributor. During this period, he and Ron developed the flamboyant **Fez Whatley** character.

The original premise contained a long-running contradiction: Fez was presented as a flamboyant gay character while Todd publicly maintained that he himself was straight. The joke when listeners asked whether he was actually gay was that he was gay only during the hours of the broadcast.

In 2012, Fez later explained that this distinction eventually became psychologically difficult because he came to understand that he actually was gay. That later disclosure radically changes the way the early character reads in hindsight, but it should not be projected backward as though Todd necessarily understood all of that in 1990.

## Character and person begin to collapse

One of the central themes of the entire show is already visible here:

- Todd performs Fez.
- Listeners interact with Fez.
- Staff know Todd.
- Ron knows both.
- Todd's life influences Fez.
- The Fez persona influences Todd's life.

Over time, the distinction becomes much harder to draw.

This is not merely a biographical curiosity. The later *Ron & Fez* show repeatedly turns the unstable boundary between **person** and **radio character** into its richest material.

## Ron's early ensemble instinct

The older *Ron & Ron* production environment also helped Ron develop a skill that would define the later show: recognizing that an interesting person could be more valuable than a written bit.

A person did not need to be a professional comedian. They needed something Ron could work with:

- a strange worldview;
- an unusual voice;
- irrational confidence;
- defensiveness;
- a tendency to contradict themselves;
- a spectacular temper;
- obsessive interests;
- an odd family dynamic;
- a complete inability to explain an ordinary mistake.

Later staff such as Billy Staples, Black Earl, East Side Dave, Chris Stanley, and even Fez himself would all be used differently, but this same selection instinct runs through them.

## End of *Ron & Ron*

Ron Diaz's wife Debbie became seriously ill and died in 1995. Diaz later left the program amid major personal and corporate changes, and *Ron & Ron* ended in 1997.

Ron Bennington's next major choice was consequential: rather than finding another conventional radio personality, he elevated Fez from the supporting ecosystem into the center of the show.

That decision is almost a manifesto for what *Ron & Fez* would become: **a person from the surrounding cast becomes the main story.**

## Daytona: the actual *Ron & Fez* partnership begins

Ron and Fez debuted as a team at WKRO in Daytona Beach on **August 24, 1998**.

The surviving documentary record for the Daytona period is thinner than for WNEW/XM, so claims about individual incidents should be treated cautiously. The important structural change is clear, though: Fez now had to function not simply as a recurring character but as Ron's full co-host for hours every day.

A short character can rely on a narrow premise. A co-host must provide opinions, stories, reactions, vulnerabilities, conflicts, and continuity.

That pressure helped move the program from **character comedy** toward **real-life relationship comedy**.

## The central takeaway

Before New York, three pieces of the later *Ron & Fez* machine already existed:

1. **Ron as selector/director** — he could discover funny structures inside real people.
2. **Fez as unstable character/person hybrid** — a comic persona increasingly tied to the real person performing it.
3. **community instinct** — radio could generate live events, recurring social relationships, and a world around the program rather than a passive audience.

The next major transformation happens at WNEW, where early-internet culture gives that community a permanent home.
