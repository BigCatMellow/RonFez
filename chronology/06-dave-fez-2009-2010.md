# Part 6 — 2009–2010: “The Plan,” Save Dave, and the end of the classic XM ensemble

## Summary

The idea that East Side Dave might replace Fez worked as a storyline because the structural evidence was visible to everyone: Dave was talking more, creating more bits, producing the show, developing outside SiriusXM work, and competing directly with Fez while Fez often struggled to participate.

What has **not** been established is an actual secret Ron/SiriusXM plan to replace Fez with Dave.

The real twist is that Dave left first, largely over money and workload, while Fez remained.

## Why “The Plan” felt believable

By 2009:

- the program had expanded to four hours;
- Black Earl was gone;
- Dave was a major on-air presence;
- Dave had recurring characters and bits;
- Board Gossip connected him directly to the fan ecosystem;
- Dave competed with Fez in formal games;
- Dave had *Special Delivery* with Sam Roberts;
- Fez was increasingly inconsistent.

From Fez's perspective, the younger producer could plausibly look like someone occupying the space of a co-host even without a formal management scheme.

## Siren Series and Character Showdown

The Fez/Dave rivalry was formalized through trivia competitions and the 2009 **Character Showdown**.

These were comic games on the surface but carried a deeper implication:

**Who is the stronger radio performer right now?**

The question became especially uncomfortable because Fez could still demonstrate deep character skill in those contests. The issue was not that the ability had vanished. It was that he could not reliably access that energy in ordinary daily radio.

## Current forensic position on “The Plan”

### Strongly supported

- Dave wanted airtime.
- Dave had become extremely important to the program.
- Dave sometimes functioned like a third host.
- Fez appeared threatened by Dave on-air.
- Ron deliberately used the rivalry for content.
- fans chose sides.

### Not established

- an actual SiriusXM management decision to fire Fez and install Dave;
- a secret Ron/Dave agreement to force Fez out.

The best current classification is:

**literal corporate Plan: unproven / likely storyline**

**underlying Fez insecurity: understandable and plausibly genuine**

## Anger as a way to unlock Fez

One of the strangest production discoveries of late R&F was that Fez could be quiet for long stretches and then become fast, funny, and highly verbal when angered.

That created a difficult incentive:

`Fez silent → antagonize Fez → active Fez returns → better radio`

From a production standpoint, it worked.

From a human standpoint, it raised obvious ethical questions, especially in light of what Fez later disclosed about anxiety, depression, and identity conflict.

## Dave's workload

Dave was not only a performer. He still had production responsibilities.

By 2010, contemporary fan/commentary accounts described a shrinking production staff, a large on-air role, outside radio work, a long commute, a young family, and financial pressure.

This matters because the mythology said:

**Dave is rising toward Fez's chair.**

The practical reality became:

**Dave cannot afford to remain in Dave's own chair under the current arrangement.**

## Save Dave

In spring 2010, Dave publicly sought more money from SiriusXM. Contemporary coverage reported that he said he would leave unless the pay situation improved.

Fans responded with the **Save Dave** campaign using early Twitter-era organizing and hashtags such as:

- `#SaveDave`
- `#SaveESD`
- `#DavePound`

Opie publicly supported Dave.

This is an important technological marker because the fan community was beginning to move some of its organizing function from RonFez.net/Paltalk to **Twitter**.

## Davepound and Whatley Posse

Named fan factions made the Fez/Dave rivalry quasi-political:

- **The Davepound**
- **The Whatley Posse**

The in-studio conflict and the outside fan conflict mirrored one another, creating another feedback loop.

## Twitter migration

By 2010 the R&F ecosystem had begun experimenting with real-time Twitter activity, including an online Super Bowl party through the @202Friends account.

The cultural change was significant:

- RonFez.net was a **place**;
- Twitter was a **network**.

Centralized boards produced deep repeated contact among the same users. Social networks improved distribution but diluted some of that town-square density.

## Shorty Awards / llama incident

In March 2010, the R&F archive preserved Dave crashing the Shorty Awards stage after losing to a llama.

The incident is useful because it demonstrates how far Dave's character identity had spread outside the primary show. A bizarre event became believable precisely because it fit the established logic of “Dave.”

## *Special Delivery*

Dave and Sam Roberts's *Special Delivery* provided Dave with an independent SiriusXM identity.

His path had become:

`fan/orbiter → employee → major character → outside host`

This is perhaps the clearest example of the R&F audience-to-creator pipeline producing an actual broadcaster.

## Chris “Pepper Hicks” Stanley waiting underneath

Chris Stanley had already become a recognizable on-air personality before Dave left. By the end of 2009, outside commentary was explicitly treating him as one of Channel 202's distinctive voices.

His style was nearly opposite Dave's:

- Dave pushed into the center;
- Chris could be funny from the edge;
- Dave announced stories;
- Chris often had stories extracted from him;
- Dave sought conflict;
- Chris tended toward dry resistance or minimalism.

That difference becomes essential after Dave's departure.

## Dave leaves

East Side Dave departed *Ron & Fez* in **June 2010** after the pay dispute was not resolved to his satisfaction.

The feared succession never happened.

Fez remained.

Chris Stanley moved into the executive-producer role.

## Why Dave's departure mattered structurally

Removing Dave removed more than one employee.

The show lost:

- a major Ron foil;
- Fez's principal antagonist;
- constant character generation;
- outside adventures and social stories;
- Board Gossip;
- the strongest direct bridge between staff drama and fan drama;
- a performer willing to expose almost anything for content.

Contemporary listeners immediately noticed the hole.

Dave had become **structural**, not merely entertaining.

## Fez's third heart attack

In September 2010, Fez suffered another documented heart attack.

The sequence around 2009–10 is therefore important:

- January 2009 — Earl leaves;
- 2009 — Dave/Fez rivalry becomes central;
- May 2009 — show expands to four hours;
- 2009–10 — Fez participation remains inconsistent;
- spring 2010 — Dave pay dispute;
- June 2010 — Dave leaves;
- September 2010 — Fez suffers another heart attack.

This is a reasonable point to mark the **end of the classic early-XM ensemble**, even though the show continued for five more years.

## Fan civilization at the transition

RonFez.net remained enormous. Surviving snapshots from around 2010 show a site with tens of thousands of registered members, tens of thousands of threads, and millions of posts accumulated over its lifespan.

But the internet environment around it was changing. Twitter, Facebook, YouTube, blogs, and podcasts increasingly distributed the fan population across platforms.

Dave's departure therefore coincides with both:

- the end of one production ensemble;
- the beginning of the decline of the old centralized fan town square.

## Core conclusion

Dave's era embodied the permeability of R&F more completely than almost anyone:

`fan → board/orbit personality → employee → character → creator → live-event performer → family life as content → rival to co-host → independent host → leader of fan faction`

When he left, the show became less porous.

The remaining years became smaller, quieter, and psychologically stranger.
