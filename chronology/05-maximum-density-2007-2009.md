# Part 5 — 2007–2009: maximum density, Paul O, fan hierarchy, and the secondary universe taking over

## Summary

By 2007–2009, *Ron & Fez* had developed a second program underneath the official broadcast: RonFez.net, Paltalk, parties, fan personalities, staff relationships, old Florida characters, O&A crossover, listener-created music, personal disasters, and years of accumulated grudges. The radio increasingly worked by pulling pieces of that hidden world onto the air.

This period produced extraordinary comedy and some of the most ethically complicated material in the show's history.

## The Big ASS Bash / Midnight Rider model

The 2007 Big ASS Bash at the Hard Rock Cafe illustrates how the show merged wrestling-style fiction, live events, staff conflict, and fan participation.

East Side Dave's **Midnight Rider** storyline was deliberately theatrical, but the event itself was real, involved professional wrestling figures, required actual production work, and generated genuine stress and arguments.

This is a recurring R&F pattern:

`fictional conflict → real event → genuine stress → performed reaction → genuine reaction folded back into the bit`

That makes a simple “fake or real?” question inadequate.

## Paul O

Paul O was not merely an XM-era caller. His relationship with Ron went back to the Florida *Ron & Ron* world.

He often appeared under the nominal pretext of reviewing movies, but the actual entertainment came from Ron interrupting, redirecting, invoking old history, or pulling Paul's private life into the discussion.

The recurring phrase **“Paul O, your life's a movie”** became increasingly literal as Paul's personal world became serialized radio material.

## Gail O

Paul's wife Gail became a major presence through phone calls and background interruptions. Her relationship with Paul generated enormous amounts of comedy because Ron could activate the dynamic simply by asking questions.

But the underlying marriage and personal difficulties were real.

In March 2007, Gail died. Some listeners initially questioned whether the announcement might be a work because it occurred near April Fool's Day and the show had trained its audience to distrust apparently factual announcements.

This is an important historical point: **audience uncertainty had become so deeply conditioned that even death could initially be interpreted through the work/shoot framework.**

## Paul O after Gail

Paul later returned to the show, but the dynamic had changed. Surviving fan logs from 2007 explicitly note that the calls were “not the same without Gail O.”

Paul's subsequent calls included grief, drinking, movie reviews, and continuing personal material.

This is one of the clearest examples of the show's central ethical tension: genuine vulnerability could also be compelling radio.

## *Gap*

Paul eventually made a low-budget film called ***Gap***, turning the long-running “your life's a movie” joke into an actual movie project. The show then discussed and promoted the film, creating another recursive loop:

`Paul's life becomes radio → radio jokes that his life is a movie → Paul makes movie → movie returns as radio material`

## Big A

Big A / Andrew Gold crossed between the O&A and R&F ecosystems. Later Dave retrospectives specifically discuss the different ways the two shows used him and the significance of his relationship with Fez.

This illustrates a key R&F trait: Ron often transformed a recurring outsider into **relationship continuity** rather than treating the person as a one-time spectacle.

## Stalker Patti

Stalker Patti similarly migrated between the O&A and R&F worlds. Official episode descriptions from 2007 show her visiting the studio alongside celebrity guests, old callers, Board Gossip, and new Dave characters.

The categories were intentionally weak. A celebrity, fan personality, caller, employee, and fictional bit could all occupy the same broadcast without a change in format.

## Happy Typing Girl

Happy Typing Girl / Debbie Wolf is a strong example of:

`board identity → recognized community member → real-world participant → person known to staff/listeners → possible radio subject`

RonFez.net event photography directly documents her inside the physical fan/show world.

## Paltalk as social hierarchy

Paltalk did more than provide real-time chat. It helped create **tiers of proximity**:

- ordinary listeners;
- board posters;
- Paltalk regulars;
- people known by staff;
- people who attended parties;
- people who knew Fez or Dave socially;
- people who visited the studio;
- people who became recurring radio subjects.

Recognition by the show had status value. This helped create real friendships but also cliques, feuds, and attention-seeking behavior.

## The show mocks the hierarchy while reinforcing it

Ron and Dave frequently made fun of how seriously fans took board status and interpersonal drama.

But the act of discussing those fights on national satellite radio made them **more important**, which generated more posts, which created more Board Gossip.

The show could therefore ridicule fandom obsession while simultaneously rewarding it.

## 2008: people become the format

By 2008, program descriptions often contain a string of unrelated events connected only by the recurring cast: Fez health discussion, Dave characters, Mooch questions, staff problems, odd guests, listener reactions.

The organizing principle is no longer a topic.

It is:

**“I want to know what these people do today.”**

That makes the show resemble later personality-driven podcast/streaming culture more than traditional talk radio.

## Dave as repertory company

Dave did not merely function as a chaotic producer. He generated characters, songs, announcers, wrestling personas, puppets, and recurring bits such as Guy Guyerson and Midnight Rider material.

The crucial difference from ordinary sketch comedy is that the fictional material remained attached to **Dave's real life and real humiliation**.

His performance identity and private identity fed each other.

## Fez moves in the opposite direction

During this same period, Fez increasingly struggled to participate consistently.

That created an unavoidable structural problem:

- Dave generated more material;
- Ron used what generated material;
- Dave therefore received more airtime;
- Fez could feel displaced;
- that feeling generated tension;
- the tension itself became material.

This loop would later become “The Plan.”

## Silence becomes content

By the late 2000s, there were stretches where Fez said very little.

A conventional show would hide this as production failure. R&F increasingly made the failure itself the topic:

**Why isn't Fez talking?**

Ron asked. Staff discussed it. Callers discussed it. Boards discussed it. Fez's reaction to being discussed generated additional material.

The program had learned to convert **non-performance into performance**.

## Earl leaves

Black Earl resigned in **January 2009** after roughly nine years with Ron and Fez.

This changed the chemistry considerably. Earl's low-energy resistance had given Dave a natural target and Ron an endlessly interrogable subject.

With Earl gone, Dave's most obvious remaining antagonist increasingly became **Fez**.

## Dave versus Fez

By 2009, Dave/Fez conflict became formalized through arguments, old grievances, trivia contests, and the **Siren Series**.

This wasn't merely comedy competition. Underneath the games sat an obvious question:

**Which one of these people is functionally Ron's partner?**

Fans divided into factions and debated whether Dave had saved the show or was helping destroy the original Ron/Fez dynamic.

## Performed real life

The best model for this era is not scripted reality and not pure documentary.

It is **performed real life**:

- events are often real;
- everyone knows they are being observed;
- people perform heightened versions of themselves;
- genuine emotion can appear inside a bit;
- Ron shapes the event live through conversation;
- fan reaction becomes another layer of the story.

## The fan ecosystem as proto-creator culture

By 2008 the architecture looked strikingly modern:

- daily long-form broadcast;
- message board;
- live chat/video room;
- fan clips;
- songs and remixes;
- physical meetups;
- parasocial personalities;
- fans becoming creators;
- creators becoming cast.

The major difference from modern social media was **density**. The same people repeatedly returned to the same boards, rooms, bars, and broadcasts, producing unusually strong continuity.

## Beginning of contraction

By 2009, Earl was gone, Fez was struggling, Dave was carrying more of the program, and the show expanded to four hours daily.

At the same time, internet culture itself was decentralizing away from a few centralized forums toward Twitter, Facebook, YouTube, and other platforms.

The old R&F town square had not vanished, but its peak density was beginning to pass.
