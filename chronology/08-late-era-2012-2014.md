# Part 8 — 2012–2014: Inner Voice Fez, Popcorn, Shelby, Cakehorn, and Steakgate

## Summary

Coming out did not restore the old two-man *Ron & Fez*. Instead, the show found a strange second life by turning Fez's difficulty performing into a source of new structures and characters. Interns became central cast members, Chris Stanley's orbit replaced some of the old message-board character pipeline, and Ron increasingly built hours around one psychological crack, one odd word, or one small inconsistency.

This era produced **Inner Voice Fez, D-Man, Intern Popcorn, Shelby, Cakehorn, and Steakgate**.

## Fez's Inner Voice

Fez began personifying the internal voice that told him:

- he was not funny;
- everyone disliked him;
- he was failing;
- he was embarrassing himself.

Ron responded by treating the inner voice almost as another guest. Rather than merely discussing negative self-talk, the show theatricalized it.

At times the distinction expanded into **Todd Hillier versus Fez Whatley**, effectively allowing one person to argue from different aspects of his identity.

This gave Fez something that had become difficult for him: **distance**. If simply being “Fez” was hard, he could sometimes perform the person attacking Fez.

## Inner voice terminology caution

The phrase “inner voice” should not be retroactively treated as evidence of psychosis or hallucination. In ordinary language, internal verbal thought is common. The radio device was a theatricalized version of Fez's self-critical internal dialogue.

## D-Man and character access

The late show repeatedly tested whether characters could unlock Fez's old comic machinery.

The **D-Man**, a tough-guy/biker-style personality, became one of the clearest examples. When inside a character, Fez could suddenly sound confident and fast again.

This suggests that his underlying comic ability had not simply disappeared. The harder task was **sustaining ordinary Fez Whatley as a conversational co-host**.

## Interns become the new casting pipeline

Earlier eras recruited characters through:

- callers;
- RonFez.net;
- Paltalk;
- bars and live events;
- the O&A ecosystem.

By 2012–14, the easiest source of new recurring characters was often inside SiriusXM itself:

**interns.**

Interns were ideal R&F material because they were:

- young;
- nervous;
- low-status;
- eager for careers;
- uncertain how much of what happened was a bit;
- exposed to Chris, Ron, and Fez at very different levels of authority.

## Intern Popcorn

Intern Popcorn became deeply entangled in the Fez dynamic and acquired the nickname **“Fez Jr.”**

On **May 8, 2013**, Popcorn had a memorable on-air breakdown while discussing his own problems.

The production logic was pure R&F:

Most shows: `intern in crisis → remove intern from microphone`

R&F: `intern in crisis → Ron asks questions → crisis becomes the conversation`

The same mechanism once used on Billy Staples, Earl, Dave, and Fez was now being applied to interns.

## Fez versus interns as genre

A recurring late-era structure developed:

1. Fez becomes attached to or interested in an intern.
2. The intern disappoints him.
3. Fez interprets the behavior as disrespect.
4. Ron notices that Fez's reaction is disproportionate.
5. The intern tries to explain.
6. The explanation makes Fez angrier.
7. Fez becomes highly verbal and funny.
8. callers and listeners take sides.

This was useful radio because anger often unlocked Fez.

## Shelby Royston

Shelby interned with the show in 2013 and later joined the staff.

He became one of the most important final-era additions because he was **not Dave**. Dave provoked Fez through overwhelming personality and legitimate competition for airtime. Shelby could provoke Fez through mild sarcasm, youth, or insufficient deference.

At first, Fez appeared to like Shelby considerably.

Then the relationship changed.

## Thanksgiving with Fez's family

Fez brought Shelby to his family Thanksgiving dinner. The story later became a major on-air topic.

The show jokingly framed Shelby as a “twink boyfriend” figure, but the exact emotional meaning should not be treated as literal biographical fact. What is established is that Shelby entered Fez's real family space and that the resulting awkwardness became radio material.

This is a late-era replacement for the old Friday Night Lights mechanism:

Earlier: `Fez socializes with fans → Monday show investigates`

Later: `Fez brings coworker into family life → show investigates`

The private/public boundary moved even further inward.

## Move to Raw Dog

On **January 6, 2014**, *Ron & Fez* moved away from the old O&A channel environment to Raw Dog Comedy Hits 99.

By this point the practical lineup was often:

- Ron;
- Fez;
- Chris Stanley;
- Shelby.

Listeners increasingly joked that whenever Fez was quiet, the functioning conversational show was effectively **Ron and Hicks**.

## Cakehorn

In March 2014, Fez used the word **“cakehorn”** for someone's mouth.

Ron immediately recognized the comic potential and began treating the word as an object requiring serious linguistic investigation:

- What exactly is a cakehorn?
- Is it a mouth?
- Why “horn”?
- Why “cake”?
- Can someone run a cakehorn?
- Can someone toot a cakehorn?
- Did Fez mean cakehole?

Every attempt Fez made to end the discussion generated another branch.

This is almost a perfect demonstration of Ron's late-era technique:

**find the thing someone desperately wants everyone to stop noticing, then notice it more carefully.**

## Cakehorn and audience folklore

The phrase immediately crossed into fan vocabulary and O&A-adjacent discussion. Years later, phrases such as “shut your cakehorn” or references to “tootin'” remain enough for old listeners to recognize one another.

This is how a spontaneous or semi-spontaneous radio moment becomes **folklore/password**.

## Was Cakehorn spontaneous?

This remains unresolved.

Contemporary listeners largely interpreted the original utterance as a genuine verbal mistake. Later explanations have suggested a production misunderstanding involving handwriting and the word “cakehole.” Ron has also discussed the origin in contexts—such as April Fool's Day—that make absolute certainty difficult.

Current forensic classification:

- **origin:** unresolved;
- **subsequent escalation:** unquestionably deliberate improvisation/work.

Possible model:

`accident → improvisation → performance → genuine irritation → performance built around genuine irritation`

## Shelby as Fez antagonist

Shelby's involvement in Cakehorn and other late-era incidents accelerated the change from Fez's earlier fondness toward hostility.

Shelby's strength was subtle provocation. He could needle Fez without appearing to try particularly hard.

This created a late echo of Dave/Fez, but with a very different power relationship: Shelby was a young employee/intern figure rather than a genuine rival host.

## *The Halls*

In 2014, the show produced a scripted Instagram series called ***The Halls*** about interns navigating a New York media company.

This marks another shift in fan/media architecture.

Early 2000s: `fans build internet extensions around radio`

2014: `show itself creates internet-native extension`

The secondary universe had moved from grassroots expansion toward official transmedia.

## Fragmentation of the old town square

By 2014, RonFez.net still mattered historically and socially, but the fan center had spread across:

- Twitter;
- Instagram;
- YouTube;
- Reddit and other forums;
- archive collectors;
- smaller social groups.

This increased distribution but weakened the density of one shared town square.

## Anthony Cumia fired

In July 2014, SiriusXM fired Anthony Cumia after racist social-media posts. The old O&A/R&F satellite neighborhood was therefore breaking apart institutionally at the same time the fan world was fragmenting technologically.

R&F became more self-contained.

## Steakgate

Steakgate is one of the most important late-era work/shoot cases.

The premise: Fez promised **steaks** for an intern-related occasion and returned with food from **Steak 'n Shake**, specifically steakburgers.

The argument centered on whether a steakburger counted as steak, with Fez defending the premise through increasingly elaborate logic.

The discussion then moved beyond burgers into deeper frustrations, especially between Fez and Chris Stanley.

## Work/shoot status of Steakgate

Later retrospective discussion attributes to Ron and Chris an explanation that the basic steak premise was **constructed**, but that the argument eventually touched genuine nerves and became real anger.

Current classification:

**WORK → SHOOT → WORK**

The exact sentence where performance became genuine cannot currently be established.

This is a useful teaching example because the fake premise appears to have created conditions for real conflict, after which the absurd premise helped everyone return to a performable frame.

## Chris Stanley erupts

Chris normally occupied the laid-back role, so genuine loud anger carried unusual weight.

During Steakgate, the dispute eventually touched the obvious issue everyone had been living with: **Fez's inability to participate consistently in a show bearing his name**.

This transformed the segment from burger semantics into accumulated workplace frustration.

## The late-era comedy engine

The show no longer required elaborate formats.

A tiny stimulus was enough:

- one invented word;
- one burger dispute;
- one family dinner;
- one intern's anxiety;
- one odd claim.

The people and accumulated history did the rest.

## Why 2013–14 can be considered a second golden age

The show did not return to its earlier form. The sprawling Dave/Earl/Paltalk carnival was gone.

But Ron found a different engine:

`Fez psychology + Chris resistance + interns + Shelby provocation + decades of continuity`

It was smaller, darker, and more claustrophobic, but often exceptionally rich radio.
