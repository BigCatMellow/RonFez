# Thematic File — Comedy formats, recurring bits, and how the show generated laughs

## Why this deserves its own history

*Ron & Fez* changed formats repeatedly over seventeen years, but its most durable comic structures reveal a consistent philosophy: **create a framework, let people contribute, and follow whatever becomes funniest rather than forcing a predetermined ending.**

The early show often used explicit games and recurring formats. The middle show increasingly used relationships as the format. The late show sometimes needed almost no formal premise at all; one strange word or one bad explanation could fill hours.

## Comedy Pyramid

The **Comedy Pyramid** is one of the clearest expressions of early R&F's audience-driven comedy.

### Basic structure

1. Ron establishes a topic or comic premise.
2. Callers contribute one short joke or association.
3. Ron immediately evaluates whether it works.
4. A good line can change the direction of the segment.
5. Another caller adds another “block” to the pyramid.

The caller's job was not to deliver a long story or explain an opinion. It was to understand the premise, make one contribution, and get out.

### Why it mattered

Comedy Pyramid did three things at once:

- created a large distributed writers' room;
- trained listeners in **Ron Bennington's comic rhythm**;
- selected for callers who understood how to participate without hijacking the show.

This helps explain why R&F eventually developed unusually strong recurring callers. The audience was actively learning the show's grammar.

### Example preserved in the archive

A 2002 Comedy Pyramid used the birth of Sarah Jessica Parker and Matthew Broderick's baby as the frame, with callers effectively phoning in as fictional well-wishers.

### Caller experience

Later listener recollections describe Comedy Pyramid as an unusually accessible way to call the show: give the Big ASS number, deliver the line, and either get the laugh or get dismissed.

One retrospective caller remembered suggesting **heroin** as something Fez might try for anxiety, with Ron immediately building on the line.

The exact joke matters less than the mechanism: a civilian listener can successfully add one brick to the show.

## Big ASS Prize Closet

A successful Comedy Pyramid contribution or other good call could earn access to the **Big ASS Prize Closet**.

The actual prize was often secondary. Listeners remember receiving promotional CDs, DVDs, box sets, autographed material, and miscellaneous station items—sometimes late or damaged, which itself became lore.

The real reward was **Ron laughed**.

The Prize Closet turned comic contribution into a ritualized achievement.

## Failure ritual

Bad calls could receive a quick dismissive “oooh, sorry” style response and disappear.

This established a clear game:

- you have very little time;
- the audience is listening;
- do not over-explain;
- make Ron laugh;
- leave.

The structure simultaneously encouraged participation and protected the show from long bad calls.

## Comedy Pyramid as historical marker

The Comedy Pyramid is remembered primarily as an **FM-era** format.

That change is revealing.

### Early R&F
`What can the audience bring us?`

### Middle R&F
`What are Dave, Earl, Fez, and the community doing?`

### Late R&F
`What is happening psychologically/interpersonally in this room right now?`

The show gradually stopped needing caller-generated joke frameworks because the recurring people themselves had become durable story engines.

## Fezetorials

**Fezetorials** are historically important because they preserve a version of Fez that late-era memory can obscure.

They gave Fez a structured comic-commentary format in which he was expected to **drive the performance**, rather than merely react to Ron or be analyzed by the room.

When old Fezetorial material was replayed during memorial programming after Fez's death, listeners were struck by how active, fast, and performative early Fez sounded.

A complete history should therefore use Fezetorials as evidence against the simplistic narrative that Fez was always the quiet or damaged partner.

## Fictional recurring calls

Early R&F also used recurring fictional callers as flexible comedy structures.

Known examples include:

- Iris — Ron Bennington;
- Kenny Allen — Miles Bennington;
- Jan Brady — Fez;
- André the Giant — Fez;
- Mr. Rogers — Giant Brian Carothers.

The important feature is **continuity**. The audience remembers the character's biography, phrases, relationships, and rules, allowing a short call to carry much more information than a one-off impression.

## Joke-offs and contests

The show repeatedly used competitive structures to turn staff, callers, and comedians into participants.

The archive preserves examples such as the old **Enema Jokeoff**, involving the wider WNEW/O&A ecosystem and professional comedians.

These contests foreshadow later internal competitions such as Dave versus Fez.

## Drinking shows / Power Hours

The archive maintained a dedicated **Drinking Shows** category, indicating that alcohol-centered broadcasts were not isolated accidents.

Alcohol changed established roles:

- quiet people became loud;
- defensive people overshared;
- Fez could become less inhibited;
- Dave became even more theatrical;
- ordinary social control weakened.

The essential R&F ingredient was that Ron often remained sufficiently composed to **observe the collapse**.

The comedy was not simply “people get drunk.” It was:

**Ron narrates and interrogates while everyone else's role destabilizes.**

East Side Dave's Power Hours are among the most frequently remembered later examples.

## Board Gossip

**Board Gossip** formalized the secondary internet universe as a recurring content source.

Dave surveyed fan discussions, feuds, insults, rumors, and complaints, then reported them on the show.

The segment created a closed loop:

`show → board → Dave reads board → show discusses board → board reacts → next Board Gossip`

This is one of the clearest cases where the audience was not merely commenting on the show but **participating in its content-production cycle**.

## Character Showdowns

By 2009, some of the early Comedy Pyramid logic had turned inward.

Instead of callers building a joke, Ron could pit **Fez and Dave** against each other in formal character competitions.

The archive preserves multiple rounds with impressions/characters such as:

- Jan Brady;
- André the Giant;
- Mike Francesa;
- Nature Boy;
- various Dave creations.

The comedy game also carried an uncomfortable subtext:

**Is Dave becoming a more active performer than Fez?**

Thus a simple impression contest simultaneously functioned as comedy, workplace competition, and serialized drama.

## Siren Series / trivia rivalry

Fez and Dave also competed through recurring trivia formats involving subjects such as Batman and *Star Wars*.

These contests turned a real or perceived professional rivalry into formal entertainment and gave fan factions a way to take sides.

## Simulcasts

R&F also used **simulcast-style commentary**, in which the show supplied an alternative audio experience around public events.

Examples discussed in the originating conversation include:

- Tiger Woods' public apology;
- Academy Awards / Oscar simulcasts.

These deserve a dedicated event/media file because they combine broadcast commentary, communal live listening, and event culture.

## Games with Chris and Shelby

Late-era games such as **Guess the Celebrity** and **What Happened First** became effective because the cast relationships were already well established.

The game rules were often less important than:

- Chris's reaction;
- Shelby's confidence or mistakes;
- Fez's irritation;
- Ron's ability to turn a wrong answer into a larger conversation.

The formal game became a trigger for relationship comedy.

## Inner Voice / identity-as-format

Late R&F sometimes turned parts of Fez's internal experience into quasi-characters:

- Inner Voice Fez;
- Todd versus Fez;
- D-Man;
- other character structures that allowed Fez to perform around the difficulty of simply being himself on-air.

This represents the late show's most psychologically unusual format: **a host's difficulty performing becomes a performance framework.**

## Cakehorn as accidental-format model

Cakehorn is almost the purest late-era example of Ron needing no pre-built game.

One odd word appears.

Ron investigates it.

The room contributes.

Fez resists.

The resistance creates more questions.

The audience adopts the language.

The segment can then recur without any formal rules.

This demonstrates how far the show had evolved from Comedy Pyramid. The basic improvisational principle remained the same, but **the premise could now be microscopic**.

## Steakgate as work/shoot format

Steakgate appears to have begun with a constructed premise—Fez bringing Steak 'n Shake steakburgers after promising “steaks”—before touching genuine workplace frustration.

Its importance is not simply the hamburger argument. It demonstrates how a comic framework could create enough safety/structure for real conflict to emerge and then give everyone a path back into performance.

Current forensic shorthand:

**WORK → SHOOT → WORK**

## Failure as a recurring format

One of Ron's deepest discoveries was that a failed premise can outperform a successful one.

Examples:

- Skippy the cat failing to demonstrate the promised behavior;
- Fez failing to explain an inconsistency;
- Earl failing to reconcile a story;
- a planned segment collapsing;
- an intern losing control;
- a co-host failing to talk.

The general structure:

`claim/plan → failure → Ron notices failure → explanation → deeper failure → comedy`

This is one of the most transferable descriptions of Ron's hosting method.

## Overall evolution of R&F comedy

### Stage 1 — structured participatory comedy
Comedy Pyramid, Fezetorials, fictional callers, contests.

### Stage 2 — ensemble relationship comedy
Billy, Earl, Dave, staff, callers, message-board people.

### Stage 3 — serialized social-world comedy
Board Gossip, Paltalk feuds, weddings, parties, private relationships.

### Stage 4 — psychological/interpersonal micro-comedy
Fez's silence, Inner Voice, Shelby, Cakehorn, Steakgate, Skippy.

## Central conclusion

Comedy Pyramid may be the most historically revealing early bit because it contains the DNA of the later show:

- establish a premise;
- invite contributions;
- evaluate instantly;
- build rather than finish;
- follow the funniest unexpected direction;
- let the audience participate.

Over time, *Ron & Fez* stopped needing formal pyramids because **the entire social universe had become one giant continuously building comedy pyramid**.
