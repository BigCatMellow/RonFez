# Thematic File — Music, deep tracks, rejoiners, openers, and the sonic identity of *Ron & Fez*

## Why music belongs in the history

Music was not incidental bumper material on *Ron & Fez*. The originating retrospective repeatedly returned to the idea that Ron and the production team took pride in finding **great songs, deep tracks, unexpected selections, and music that said something about the moment**.

A complete reconstruction therefore needs to treat music as its own layer of the show:

- opening sequences;
- closing songs;
- rejoiners/bumper music;
- deep-track discovery;
- songs used as coded commentary;
- songs linked to specific characters or incidents;
- music used to punctuate emotional moments;
- listener-created songs;
- staff-built sound collages;
- artists appearing in studio;
- later Ron projects that make his curatorial role more explicit.

This file preserves everything developed so far and establishes the structure for a much deeper future song-by-song index.

## Ron as music curator

One of the user's explicit memories is that the show **took pride in discovering great songs and deep tracks**.

That fits the documented behavior of the program:

- songs were selected for thematic meaning rather than random filler;
- attentive listeners interpreted music choices as clues;
- the show used unusual or less-obvious tracks as openers/rejoiners;
- music discussion was part of Ron's broader cultural identity;
- Ron later hosted programming explicitly centered on deeper music discovery.

A future research pass should avoid reducing the history to only the most famous songs. The obscure rejoiner heard once in 2006 is exactly the kind of detail this archive is intended to preserve.

## 2005 WJFK exit: music as coded communication

One of the clearest examples of music doing narrative work came during the final WJFK period.

As rumors of the move to XM intensified, listeners noticed increasingly suggestive song choices. The repeated use of **“I Shall Be Released”** became especially associated with the final terrestrial period.

The important point is not merely that Ron played a song he liked.

The audience understood the music as possible **communication around something the hosts could not yet state directly**.

That created another R&F feedback system:

`song choice → attentive fan notices → board interpretation → rumor → show awareness of rumor → more suggestive music`

Music therefore became part of the work/shoot game.

## “Satellite of Love”

Lou Reed's **“Satellite of Love”** became one of the most emotionally important songs associated with the show.

It worked on several levels:

- literal connection to the satellite-radio era;
- established closing/identity association;
- emotional resonance during the final 2015 goodbye;
- renewed resonance during Fez memorial programming after his death.

Fans specifically remembered singing/crying along with it during the final show and reacting strongly when it appeared during memorial programming.

The song effectively became part of the show's ritual language.

## Sylvester — “You Make Me Feel (Mighty Real)”

After Fez publicly came out in 2012, the show used Sylvester's **“You Make Me Feel (Mighty Real)”** as a musical punctuation mark.

This is a textbook R&F music cue:

- the underlying moment is emotionally significant;
- the song is thematically exact;
- the cue adds a joke without erasing the seriousness;
- the title itself comments on the tension between persona and authentic identity.

The moment demonstrates Ron's instinct for using music as **editorial commentary**.

## “North American Scum” and the XM-era openers

The show became associated with elaborate XM-era opening sequences incorporating music, movie/TV dialogue, and pop-culture drops, with **LCD Soundsystem's “North American Scum”** becoming part of the sonic identity discussed in the originating retrospective.

The surviving show history credits **Mooch Cassidy and Chris Stanley** with constructing elaborate openings.

These were not simply intros. They functioned like compact audio collages:

- music selection;
- movie dialogue;
- television clips;
- topical references;
- staff humor;
- tone-setting before Ron even began the day's conversation.

A future archive should preserve the openings separately because they are a form of production art that can disappear when fans save only the spoken segments.

## Mooch Cassidy and production music

Mooch Cassidy is important to the music history because his contribution was not merely social-character material. He participated in the **sonic construction** of the program.

This is another example of the secondary universe contributing actual production:

- songs;
- openers;
- edits;
- drops;
- fan-created music;
- staff-created audio montage.

A complete history should treat these contributors alongside the more obvious microphone personalities.

## Rejoiners / bumper music

The user specifically remembers the show's pride in finding great music and deep tracks. The most valuable future research project may therefore be a **rejoiner-by-rejoiner catalog**.

For every surviving broadcast, ideally record:

1. date;
2. segment before break;
3. song used returning from break;
4. artist;
5. exact portion used if identifiable;
6. whether Ron commented on it;
7. whether it was selected by Ron, producer, or unknown;
8. thematic relationship to the preceding/following conversation;
9. whether the song was new, obscure, classic, or deep catalog;
10. whether listeners discussed it on the boards;
11. whether it recurred often enough to become show vocabulary.

This is deliberately obsessive. The music choices are exactly the kind of detail that disappears first in clipped archives.

## Deep tracks as identity

A “deep track” generally means a song outside the most familiar hit singles—often an album cut, overlooked track, live version, B-side, or less obvious song by a known artist.

For R&F, choosing those tracks did several things:

- signaled taste rather than generic radio programming;
- rewarded attentive listeners;
- created discovery moments;
- let Ron communicate mood indirectly;
- distinguished the show from standard station bumper libraries.

The music culture should therefore be investigated as part of **Ron's editorial voice**.

## Ronnie Spector — 2011

When a planned New York Christmas appearance ran into venue trouble, Ronnie Spector performed inside SiriusXM for Ron, Fez, and a small invited group of fans.

This event combines three major R&F themes:

- Ron's genuine music interest;
- unusual access to artists;
- the porous fan/studio community.

It should eventually receive both an event entry and a music-performance entry.

## Music as emotional punctuation

R&F frequently allowed comedy and sincerity to occupy the same segment, and music was one of the best tools for managing that transition.

Important examples already identified:

- **“I Shall Be Released”** — coded/anticipatory departure meaning in 2005;
- **“You Make Me Feel (Mighty Real)”** — Fez coming-out punctuation;
- **“Satellite of Love”** — closing/farewell/memorial identity;
- **“North American Scum”** — XM-era opening identity.

Future research should recover dozens or hundreds more examples rather than treating these four as the entire music story.

## Ron's later music work as supporting context

The originating retrospective noted that Ron later built programming around **Deep Tracks**-style music discovery, reinforcing the idea that the musical curation heard throughout R&F reflected a genuine long-term interest rather than random production habit.

A complete history of *Ron & Fez* cannot be reconstructed from spoken bits alone.

The music acted as:

- mood;
- joke;
- clue;
