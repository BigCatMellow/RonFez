# The Living Story of *Ron & Fez*

> **A compiled oral history, not a finished verdict.**
>
> This page braids together the surviving broadcasts, contemporary web records, physical artifacts, later participant testimony, fan memory, photographs, event archives and forensic reconstruction collected in this repository. It is written to **read like oral history**, but it is not a fabricated transcript. Speaker-labeled passages below are explicitly **paraphrases** of what those people or source communities have said in the underlying record unless quotation marks and a source indicate otherwise.
>
> Where memories conflict, the conflict stays in the story. Where we do not know, the story says so.

---

# Prologue — There were always two shows

If you only have clips, *Ron & Fez* can be difficult to explain.

A man named Sheepy appears and everybody already knows how to react to him. Somebody mentions GVac or French Bread Pizza as if they are public figures. East Side Dave reports an argument from a message board that half the audience apparently read before the show began. A producer's wedding becomes a major live event. A woman from Paltalk is discussed as casually as a professional comedian. Fez is furious about something that happened in a bar three nights earlier. Ron asks one ordinary question and forty minutes later an employee is trying to explain a contradiction that did not seem important when the conversation began.

To a new listener, it can sound as if half the episode is missing.

In a sense, it is.

The official broadcast was only one layer. The other layer existed on RonFez.net, in bars, in Paltalk, in staff offices, on rival boards, at weddings and boxing events, in fan songs and photographs, in the private friendships that became public stories, and in years of accumulated memory shared by listeners who knew what a screen name meant before Ron ever said it aloud.

By the show's most socially dense years, the system looked something like this:

`RADIO ↔ STAFF ↔ CALLERS ↔ BOARDS ↔ PALTALK ↔ EVENTS ↔ FRIENDSHIPS ↔ RADIO`

There was no clean beginning or end to the loop.

The remarkable part is that the loop survived the radio show. After 2015 the audience gradually stopped being a production system and became a preservation system. After Fez died in 2021, it became a memorial community. By the middle of the 2020s, old staff, fans and archives were collectively reconstructing what had happened.

This is that story.

# I. Before *Ron & Fez*: Ron learns what a radio world can be

The story begins in Florida, before the title *Ron & Fez* existed.

Ron Bennington had worked as a stand-up comic and ran a comedy club before radio became his principal medium. In 1987 he partnered with Ron Diaz on **The Ron & Ron Show** at WYNF in Tampa. The program became large enough to syndicate and to draw enormous crowds to public events.

The important inheritance was not a particular bit. It was an operating idea: **a radio show could be an ensemble and a social world rather than two hosts taking calls about a topic.**

People around the show mattered. Events mattered. A recurring person could become more valuable than a prepared sketch. A listener who knew the history heard more than a listener arriving cold.

Todd Hillier entered this environment around 1990 and became **Fez Whatley**.

The original Fez was a flamboyant gay comic persona performed by a man who publicly understood and presented himself as straight. For years, that separation was part of the premise. Decades later Fez would explain that this distinction became much more complicated when he came to understand that he actually was gay.

It is important not to rewrite the beginning using the ending. The available record does not support the idea that Todd necessarily understood his sexuality in 1990 the way he understood it in 2012. But in hindsight the Fez character introduced the central instability that would define the entire partnership:

**Where does the performed person stop and the real person begin?**

### FEZ, later account — paraphrased

The character had once given him cover. He could behave flamboyantly and the joke was that this was Fez, not Todd. Once that distinction stopped feeling true to him, the same character could become frightening rather than freeing.

### THE ARCHIVE

That later explanation belongs near the beginning because Fez's career is not a story of a naturally silent man slowly deteriorating. Early Fez was fast, aggressive, inventive, willing to do characters, editorials and impressions, and able to carry large sections of radio. Any history that remembers only “Silent Fez” erases the performer Ron originally chose as his partner.

*Ron & Ron* ended in 1997. Ron Bennington did not replace Ron Diaz with another conventional radio star. Instead, a figure from the surrounding ensemble moved into the center.

Ron and Fez began broadcasting together at WKRO in Daytona Beach on **August 24, 1998**.

That decision established a pattern the later show would repeat constantly:

`person from the edge → recurring presence → relationship develops → person becomes central`

East Side Dave would travel part of that route. So would Chris Stanley. So would dozens of civilians who never became formal co-hosts but nevertheless became characters in the world.

# II. WNEW: the audience gets a memory

On **February 21, 2000**, *Ron and Fez Dot Com* debuted on WNEW in New York.

The station itself was in a strange period: a legendary rock frequency turning into FM talk, with Opie & Anthony becoming the gravitational center of a new radio ecosystem. Ron and Fez entered that world with a nominal “Dot Com” concept built around internet material.

The internet mattered, but not in the way that title suggested.

What changed the show was not browsing websites on-air. What changed it was that the audience eventually built a permanent place to live **between broadcasts**.

Before that fully happened, the WNEW staff already demonstrated what Ron's show could do to ordinary job descriptions.

Billy Staples was a phone screener. Al Dukes was a producer. Black Earl was a production employee. Those descriptions became increasingly irrelevant because the comedy came from the people themselves.

Billy's injuries, drinking, ambitions and explanations became serialized material. Al's routines, discomfort and resistance became material. Earl's attempts to explain himself became an entire comic mechanism.

### RON'S METHOD — reconstructed from years of examples

Ron did not need to invent the underlying event. Often he needed only to notice the useful contradiction.

A person said something slightly strange.

Ron asked why.

The person clarified.

The clarification created a second problem.

Ron asked about that.

The conversation kept narrowing until the person was trapped inside their own explanation.

The best shorthand developed by this project is:

> **Ron orchestrated the frame more often than the underlying event.**

This is important because two myths fail in opposite directions. Ron was not a mastermind scripting every emotional breakdown in advance. But he was also not a passive observer who merely happened to be present when funny things occurred. His skill was recognizing the structure quickly enough to turn life into radio in real time.

## Real callers and fake callers shared the same neighborhood

The WNEW-era show also trained listeners to live with reality ambiguity.

Iris was not a naturally eccentric old woman who somehow found the show. **Ron performed Iris.** Kenny Allen was a character voiced by Ron's son Miles. Fez performed recurring characters such as Jan Brady and André the Giant. Giant Brian Carothers performed Mr. Rogers.

These fictional callers appeared in a universe that also contained real callers and increasingly real civilians.

The show did not always stop and label the category for newcomers.

Long before Steakgate or The Plan, the audience was learning a rule:

**You are responsible for knowing what kind of reality you are listening to.**

# III. RonFez.net: the town square opens

The strongest current evidence places the institutional launch of **RonFez.net around February 5, 2001**. A tenth-anniversary post from 2011 points directly back to that date, while some founding-era memories loosely reach into “2000/01,” leaving room for precursor activity or fuzzy memory.

What is not fuzzy is how quickly the place became a culture.

Within months, surviving threads already contain recurring language, signatures and adjacent fan identities. People talked about the show, but they also talked about movies, relationships, technology, politics, sports and ordinary life.

That distinction is the key to understanding RFNet.

People did not go there only to discuss *Ron & Fez*.

They went there to discuss life **with people they knew because of *Ron & Fez*.**

Years later, surviving site statistics reported roughly **38,000 registered members, 84,000 threads and more than three million posts**. Those numbers do not equal daily active users, but they establish scale and persistence.

A handle could acquire tens of thousands of posts and a decade of history. “Snoogans,” “GVac,” “spoon,” “Hottub,” “JustJon” or “Mikeyboy” could stop being usernames in the ordinary sense and become compressed biographies.

## Who ran it?

The site's exact launch-day ownership remains under reconstruction, but one important part is no longer mysterious.

In a contemporary October 2002 post, **FoundryMusicJeff / Jeff Shain** described how he ran RonFez.net, called it his board, and referred to software being used elsewhere as his board software. That is strong direct-period evidence that Jeff was a principal operational and technical figure.

It does **not** yet prove that he alone founded or legally owned every part of RFNet. JustJon appears at the site's beginning and later as uploader, news poster and event organizer. Mikeyboy later identifies himself as a former administrator and became one of the archive's great visual custodians. Other moderators and technical figures remain under reconstruction.

The important correction is that RFNet was not an accidental ungoverned fan page. It had staff, moderation, culture and software history almost from the start.

## The Big ASS Card

The show also gave the community a deliberately absurd membership ritual: the **Big ASS Card / All Secret Society**.

Listeners had numbers. They could identify themselves by number when calling. The card could have practical contest or sponsor uses, but its lasting purpose was social.

It said:

**I am from here.**

People would still reference Big ASS numbers during the show's final years, Fez memorials and later reunions.

# IV. September 11, 2001: when the network had to become serious

On the evening of September 11, Ron and Fez were on the air in New York.

The program necessarily stopped being normal comedy radio. Listeners called with fear, information, reactions and attempts to understand what had happened.

RonFez.net later preserved the **entire broadcast** as an explicit exception to its usual practice of archiving shorter material.

That choice matters.

The fan community was only months old, yet it already understood that it was responsible for preserving something larger than a funny clip.

The same pattern appeared again during **Hurricane Isabel on September 18, 2003**, when a U.S. government record later described Ron and Fez devoting the program to storm coverage and using callers as a source of local information about outages and conditions.

The system normally used for absurdity could become:

`listener → local report → Ron & Fez → everyone listening`

The audience infrastructure was useful because it already existed.

## New York Forever

After September 11, lower-Manhattan gatherings and New York Forever-style bar crawls gave the community a physical dimension. Board users met each other. Hosts and staff met listeners. Stories created in bars could move back to the board and eventually the radio.

The off-air show was beginning.

# V. 2002: the internet becomes a physical civilization

If you want one year that demonstrates how quickly the community became real, 2002 is unusually revealing.

On **February 22**, the first **Big ASS Night of Fights** took place at Downtown Bar & Club in Farmingdale. A surviving physical ticket fixes the date, venue, address, 5 p.m. doors, 21+ restriction and even the ominous fine print that the ticket did not guarantee admission.

The event wrapped real people and real employment history in professional-wrestling storytelling. Al Dukes' actual departure from the regular producer position was presented through an exit arc involving Rory Hamptons and fight-night mythology. The broader Al/Gay Randy material produced one of the show's most perfect anti-disclaimers:

**“THIS IS NOT A BIT.”**

For *Ron & Fez*, insisting that something was not a bit could make listeners more suspicious rather than less.

Three months later, on **May 17**, Big ASS Night of Fights II produced one of the best surviving censuses of the early universe.

There was French Bread Pizza years before the mature Paltalk period. Hard Rock Johnny. Billy Staples and his Diggstown-style challenge. Black Earl. Al. Anthony Cumia. Eddie Trunk. Sideshow performers. Fan volunteers.

And there was **GVac versus Matty Fridays for the RonFez.net Championship**.

That detail is historically enormous precisely because it sounds trivial.

RonFez.net was only about fifteen months into its working institutional history. Yet two message-board civilians were already recognizable enough to be placed into a live fight for a fake board championship in front of an audience expected to understand why it mattered.

The internet had become casting.

## The first RFNet Christmas party

The restored gallery also preserves a **Christmas Party 2002** category. A later discussion of a labeled photograph identifies it as the **first RF.NET X-Mas party** and remembers at least two additional group shots surviving in the archive.

The venue is remembered as Dave & Buster's at the Palisades mall, but that remains later participant/community memory rather than a fully confirmed contemporary fact.

The labeled image is already important because it begins reconstructing a founding-generation physical roster: names identified in the discussion include **HordeKing, Stalker Patti, Hosp and Moshin**.

The old File Library separately contains highly downloaded artifacts called **CyberSoldier Holiday Party Pics 2002**, **The Group Photo** and **Moshin's Tattoo**.

It is tempting to connect all of these immediately.

The project does not.

The Christmas-party group shots are now a strong candidate context for *The Group Photo*. CyberSoldier may have photographed, hosted or otherwise contributed to the 2002 holiday material. Moshin is now physically anchored to that generation.

But the direct old file IDs and page bridges remain missing.

That is what “living history” means here: the story can get narrower without becoming falsely certain.

# VI. Fairfax: exile becomes laboratory

WNEW's talk experiment collapsed after the broader fallout surrounding Opie & Anthony in 2002. Ron and Fez survived, moving to **WJFK in Fairfax, Virginia in June 2003**.

It would be easy to describe Fairfax as the awkward bridge between New York and XM.

That would be a mistake.

The show became geographically smaller and socially more permeable.

A local listener could realistically move through a pipeline like:

`listener → caller → repeat caller → event regular → studio visitor → friend of show → recurring character`

Perrynoid, Crazy Jen, Cigar Sid/Sidcada, Don the Hypnotist, Mikeyboy and others belong to this world in different ways.

### PERRYNOID — participant self-understanding, paraphrased

The identity was not simply his unfiltered private self. He later described “playing” Perrynoid. He was a real fan consciously constructing a heightened version of himself, then sending songs and material back into the show.

That category—**real person performing a radio self**—would become increasingly important.

## The fans keep New York listening

The WJFK move also created a technical problem. The old New York audience could not simply turn on the same terrestrial station anymore.

The community improvised.

Later listeners remember **Big ASS Antenna, Live365, Radio BBQ, Winamp-style streams, emailed MP3s from Msteels/Steels**, and even physically wiring computer output into a tape deck.

The fan network was now doing something remarkable:

**it was distributing the show.**

The secondary universe was not only social and archival infrastructure. During the Fairfax years, it was at least partly broadcast infrastructure.

## Events become ordinary life

The WJFK years produced pool tournaments, Caddyshack Movie Night, poker events, St. Pat's Spat, local-band showcases, charity runs, bar culture and other events in which “fan,” “friend,” “contestant” and “character” blurred.

One particularly important branch was ***The Hideout***. J-Dubs/Justin Johnson moved from the R&F/WJFK world into that program, whose own fans—the Heretics—built message boards, produced media and interacted with the show.

The participatory culture reproduced itself.

# VII. Friday Night Lights: Fez holds court

By 2005, Fez was gathering with listeners and friends on Friday nights at a Fairfax bar called **Thursdays**. The tradition became known as **Friday Night Lights**.

This was not a normal station remote.

It was closer to a weekly clubhouse.

Later memory describes Fez **“holding court.”** The phrase is especially striking because a 2007 RFNet event photograph would later be captioned **“Fez holds court with Leticia's crew.”** Different events, different years, same remembered social role.

This matters because late Silent Fez can distort the entire biography.

Fez was once not only an active broadcaster but the social center of recurring fan gatherings.

Friday Night Lights also created an almost perfect Ron Bennington story machine.

Ron generally was not there.

So Friday could produce an incident, Saturday and Sunday could produce rumors and board versions, and Monday could begin with Ron asking what actually happened.

Every participant had a slightly different account.

That is exactly the environment in which Ron's interrogative style works best.

The final Friday Night Lights occurred on **July 29, 2005**, the same day as the final WJFK broadcast. Ron is remembered as attending that final gathering.

One civilization ended and another was about to begin.

# VIII. XM: remove the clock, remove the borders

Ron and Fez began regular XM broadcasts on **September 12, 2005**.

The obvious difference was language freedom. The deeper difference was **time**.

Earl Douglas later emphasized that satellite freed the show from the rigid clocks of terrestrial radio. If something was interesting, Ron could stay with it.

That freedom was perfectly suited to a show whose best moments often began with an explanation going wrong.

One question could become forty-five minutes.

One staff dispute could become a week.

One social event could remain in continuity for years.

## Fez's first major heart attack

On **November 9, 2005**, shortly after the XM move, Fez suffered a heart attack. It was the beginning of a long sequence of serious health problems that must remain part of any responsible account of the later decline.

The show was becoming freer and larger at the same moment one of its hosts was becoming physically more vulnerable.

## East Side Dave arrives

East Side Dave McDonald joined the staff in late 2005.

Dave would become perhaps the most complete example of R&F permeability.

He was employee, fan/orbiter, producer, character, musician, wrestler, antagonist, husband whose family life became radio, leader of a fan faction, independent host and eventually oral historian.

Dave initially expected to contribute more conventional sketches. Ron discovered that Dave himself was the more useful piece of material.

### DAVE, later oral-history framing — paraphrased

He came in with ideas about bits and scripts. The actual show kept finding comedy in who he was, what he cared about, how hard he reacted and how willingly he would make his own life public.

Where Earl often tried to get out of the spotlight, Dave charged into it.

This made **Dave versus Earl** almost automatically productive.

Real workplace frustration appears to have existed. The show then ritualized and exaggerated it.

That is classic R&F:

`SHOOT → WORK → new SHOOT → more WORK`

# IX. Paltalk and Board Gossip: the audience moves inside the studio

RonFez.net had given the audience memory.

**Paltalk gave it simultaneity.**

The surviving Paltalk listing for the **Ron and Fez Big ASS Room** describes it as **created by the fans for the fans**, carries a “NO BASHING” rule, and lists **French Bread Pizza** as owner.

Paltalk could function as a parallel audience room while the broadcast was happening. Users could react in real time, develop visual identities through webcams, and in some periods become visible enough to the show that the room itself entered the broadcast.

The loop tightened:

`Ron says something → Paltalk reacts → staff sees reaction → reaction reaches radio → Paltalk reacts again`

Then East Side Dave formalized the broader internet ecology with **Board Gossip**.

A later compilation points to a failed or partial start on **November 6, 2006** and a full debut on **November 8**. The complete broadcasts survive and still need primary indexing, so those debut labels remain strong leads rather than final verdicts.

By early 2007, however, Board Gossip was already recognizable enough for outside fans to quote its presentation, and official episode metadata soon identifies it as a recurring feature.

Board Gossip's genius and pathology were the same thing.

The fans discussed the show.

Dave discussed the fans discussing the show.

Ron discussed Dave's report about the fans.

The fans then discussed being discussed.

No outside topic was necessary.

The audience could generate the next day's content simply by reacting to yesterday's content.

## It was never only RFNet

The ecosystem included **Wackbag, Full Blown Aids, STI, 3rd Tier, Postwhores** and other communities, with some identities still unresolved. A 2002 cross-board discussion already shows RFNet users carrying their board citizenship into other spaces and outsiders stereotyping RFNet as the “shiny happy” or “candy coated” board.

Dave did not invent board wars in 2006.

He turned an existing social ecology into a radio segment.

# X. 2007: maximum permeability

By 2007 the cast categories barely held.

A day could contain Ron, Fez, Earl, Dave, Chris Stanley, Paul O, Bronx Johnny, a celebrity, a wrestler, Big A, Stalker Patti, Crazy Jen, a board fight and a Paltalk reference without the show treating any of those categories as fundamentally different.

The organizing principle had become:

**What are these people doing today?**

Several events make the system visible in physical form.

## Big ASS Bash — February 23, 2007

The Big ASS Bash at the Hard Rock Cafe in Times Square mixed a worked wrestling plot with actual venue logistics, actual professional wrestlers, actual staff responsibility and an actual fan audience.

The Midnight Rider storyline involved East Side Dave, masked identities and ultimately Danny Doring. Tazz participated. Earl's ticketing role later became a separate source of Dave/Earl lore.

Dave would eventually remember the Bash in terms evoking both WrestleMania and *The Last Waltz*—half spectacle, half gathering of the whole world.

That description fits the evidence.

## Dave and Casey's wedding — June 21, 2007

Dave and Casey Elan's real wedding became an R&F event at the Hard Rock in Times Square.

A near-contemporary account says they originally considered something small and private and that Ron pushed Dave toward the much larger public event.

If that account is correct, the show did not merely cover the wedding.

It changed the wedding.

Fan photographers documented it. Mike the Teacher shot photographs. Mikeyboy and RFNet preserved material. Show-style games occurred during the event. Fez would still carry a grievance years later about not being invited to the private family dinner afterward despite helping with the public wedding.

The sequence is nearly a perfect diagram of the show:

`private relationship → radio → public spectacle → fan archive → real social consequence → future radio`

## Night of a Hundred Podcasts

Dave's later oral history remembers Ron using SiriusXM hours that might otherwise have carried Best Of programming to give friends of the show with podcasts **actual satellite airtime**.

The exact 2007 date is still unresolved, but the idea is historically important.

The fan/community pipeline had evolved from:

`listeners discussing radio`

into:

`listeners making material for radio`

into:

`listeners making their own radio/podcasts`

and finally:

`Ron giving those creators professional distribution.`

For a moment, the secondary universe was a miniature media network.

# XI. Paul O and the ethical edge

Paul O demonstrates why simple “everyone knew it was a bit” explanations fail.

Paul's relationship with Ron reached back into the Florida years. He nominally called to discuss movies, but Ron often found the real radio in Paul's life, marriage, drinking, ambitions and explanations.

“Paul O, your life's a movie” became a running frame.

Then Paul's life really did become more dramatic than the joke.

His wife **Gail O** became a major character through phone calls and marital dynamics. In March 2007 Gail died.

Some listeners initially wondered whether the death itself could be a work.

That reaction is one of the most revealing facts in the show's history.

The audience had been trained so thoroughly to distrust appearances that even a death announcement could enter the work/shoot filter.

The death was real.

Paul later returned. The calls were different. The grief was part of the record. Paul eventually made the low-budget film ***Gap***, creating another impossible loop:

`life becomes radio → radio says life is a movie → person actually makes movie → movie becomes radio`

The ethical question remains difficult because “exploitation” and “performance” do not cleanly separate. Participants often knowingly gave material to the show. The show also rewarded people for giving more of themselves than a conventional workplace or friendship might normally expose.

Both can be true.

# XII. The civilian cast

By the late 2000s, “friend of the show” described a huge middle category between ordinary listener and employee.

Some were callers. Some were board figures. Some were Paltalk regulars. Some were musicians. Some took photographs. Some attended enough events that everyone knew them. Some became genuinely close to staff. Some were inherited from O&A. Some were mostly notorious because one story would never die.

The archive now has individual or collective records for people including:

**GVac, Mikeyboy, JustJon, FoundryMusicJeff, French Bread Pizza, Happy Typing Girl, Irish Alkey, Sheepy, Bronx Johnny, Perrynoid, Crazy Jen, Big A, Fleaman, Chuckwagon/Lenny McNab, Hottub, spoon, Matty Fridays, Don Stugots, Mike the Teacher, Hard Rock Johnny, Crazed, Bobo, Snoogans, J-Dubs/Justin Johnson, Cigar Sid, Monty Love, Don the Hypnotist, Hosp/Gary Oransky, Death Metal Moe and Moshin.**

That list is still incomplete.

The important thing is not that every listener became famous.

It is that the system had **many routes by which an ordinary listener could move closer to the show**.

A useful map is:

- caller route;
- board route;
- creator route;
- event route;
- Paltalk route;
- internship route;
- relationship route.

The whole environment acted as an informal casting department.

### GVac as example

GVac/Greg Petraitis registered at RFNet in 2001, accumulated tens of thousands of posts, appeared in the physical community, became a caller/friend identity, and later participated in *Weird Medicine*. His memorial contains recollections stretching back to 2001 bar crawls and years of softball and events.

That is not “a fan who called a lot.”

It is a social life partly built inside the radio ecosystem.

### Mikeyboy as example

Mikeyboy became administrator, participant and archive custodian. Huge amounts of surviving visual history are attached to his uploads. Sometimes the photographs were taken by somebody else—Mike the Teacher, Fallon or another community member—and Mikeyboy appears to have functioned as the person who received, organized and preserved them.

The audience built its own museum while it was still living inside the thing being documented.

# XIII. Music: Ron talks even when nobody is talking

Music was never merely the thing played while the show went to break.

Ron and the production team developed a reputation for deep tracks, unexpected songs, carefully chosen rejoiners and musical commentary.

During the final WJFK period, listeners interpreted **“I Shall Be Released”** as part of the coded atmosphere surrounding the expected move to XM.

After Fez came out, Sylvester's **“You Make Me Feel (Mighty Real)”** provided a perfectly R&F form of punctuation: emotionally exact and funny at the same time.

**“North American Scum”** became associated with the later opening identity and the elaborate audio collages created around the show.

And **“Satellite of Love”** became something larger than a bumper: closing ritual, farewell language and memorial music.

There were also fan songs. Perrynoid could turn broken phone systems into reusable mythology. Sleeves and other creators contributed music that moved from audience creation into show vocabulary.

This is another way the broadcast boundary failed:

`show problem → fan creates song → song enters show → song becomes collective memory`

# XIV. Fez and Dave move in opposite directions

The late 2000s contained an increasingly uncomfortable structural fact.

Dave wanted the microphone.

Fez often could not use it.

Dave would expose himself, create a character, produce an event, fight with Earl, sing, wrestle, complain, make a family problem public or start another conflict if it could produce radio.

Fez was becoming more guarded, inconsistent and difficult to unlock.

The more Dave generated, the more airtime he received.

The more airtime Dave received, the easier it was for Fez to experience him as competition.

That tension became part of the show.

## Earl leaves

Black Earl resigned in **January 2009**. His quiet resistance had given Dave a natural antagonist and Ron one of his greatest explanation machines.

With Earl gone, Dave and Fez became more directly opposed.

Trivia competitions, character contests and the **Siren Series** could therefore work simultaneously as games and as an implicit question:

**Who is functioning as Ron's partner?**

## “The Plan”

The idea that Dave was going to replace Fez became one of the era's great stories.

The structural evidence made it believable:

- Dave talked more;
- Dave produced;
- Dave had his own characters;
- Dave had Board Gossip;
- Dave had *Special Delivery*;
- Fez was struggling;
- fans took sides.

What the project has **not** found is strong evidence of a literal secret Ron/SiriusXM/Dave agreement to remove Fez.

Current best reconstruction:

**The threat was structurally real. Fez's insecurity is understandable. The literal corporate conspiracy remains unproven and is likely storyline/lore unless stronger evidence appears.**

# XV. The irony of Save Dave

In 2010, the succession story broke in the opposite direction.

Dave's production workload, on-air role, outside work, commute, family responsibilities and compensation had become difficult to sustain. He publicly sought more money. Fans organized **Save Dave** campaigns on Twitter, using the emerging social platform in ways the old board community once would have handled internally.

The audience divided into labels such as the **Davepound** and **Whatley Posse**.

But Dave did not replace Fez.

Dave left in **June 2010**.

Chris Stanley became executive producer.

Fez remained.

Three months later, Fez suffered another heart attack.

The classic early-XM ensemble was over.

### DAVE, later retrospective position — paraphrased

The mythology suggested he was rising into Fez's chair. The practical reality was that he could not remain in his own role under the existing compensation and workload.

That distinction is essential.

# XVI. After Dave: the room gets smaller

The post-Dave show sounded different because Dave had not merely been loud. He had been **structural**.

He supplied stories, characters, antagonism, Board Gossip, outside adventures and constant public exposure.

Chris Stanley was almost his inverse.

Chris could be funny from the edge. He could wait. Ron often had to discover that Chris had a story rather than wait for Chris to announce one. He could fill space without making the show about his desire for space.

That made Chris increasingly essential to a show in which Fez's participation could not be predicted.

The central question became impossible to avoid:

**Why isn't Fez talking?**

Most radio programs would hide that production problem.

*Ron & Fez* put it on the air.

Ron asked Fez. Callers discussed it. Staff discussed it. The boards discussed it. Fez reacted to being discussed. The reaction became more material.

The attempt to fix the show became the show.

# XVII. February 24, 2012: Fez explains part of the mystery

On **February 24, 2012**, Fez publicly came out as gay.

His first-person explanation is one of the most important primary sources in the archive.

### FEZ — paraphrased from his 2012 account

When the original flamboyant Fez character was created, he understood himself as straight. Later, as he came to understand that he was gay, the character stopped feeling safely separate from the person. He began worrying that when people laughed at Fez, they might actually be laughing at him. He connected the resulting internal conflict with depression, anxiety, stage fright, panic and locking up on the air.

This explained a great deal.

It did not explain everything.

Fez had serious cardiac disease. He had anxiety and depression. He had accumulated professional insecurity. Years of behavioral patterns and public scrutiny did not disappear because one secret was finally public.

The responsible historical model is **multi-causal**.

Several oversimplifications are rejected by the evidence:

- Dave did not simply “cause” Fez.
- Ron did not obviously execute a secret plan to destroy him.
- being closeted was not the only relevant factor.
- coming out was not a magic cure.
- the health problems were not an elaborate work.

## Ron knew earlier

Contemporary accounts indicate Fez had told Ron privately years before the public announcement.

That means Ron was navigating a strange ethical constraint while listeners demanded explanations for Fez's behavior: he knew a major piece of information that was not his to disclose.

Whatever else can be debated about Ron's willingness to use Fez's difficulties as radio, one boundary matters clearly:

**Ron did not publicly out him.**

Fez made that disclosure himself.

# XVIII. The late show invents a new form out of the problem

Coming out did not restore 2003 Fez.

Instead the show developed a smaller, stranger creative system.

## Inner Voice Fez

Fez's self-critical internal dialogue became theatricalized as **Inner Voice Fez**. Ron could effectively interview the part of Fez attacking Fez.

The device matters because it gave Fez distance. If ordinary Fez was difficult to inhabit, he could sometimes perform the voice criticizing him.

## D-Man

Characters such as **D-Man** could suddenly unlock speed and confidence that ordinary conversation did not.

The ability had not simply vanished.

Access to it had become unstable.

## Interns become the new recruitment pipeline

Earlier characters arrived from phone lines, message boards, bars and Paltalk.

By 2012–2014, interns were conveniently in the building.

Intern Popcorn became “Fez Jr.” and had a memorable on-air breakdown in 2013. Shelby Royston moved from intern into staff and became one of the great final-era Fez antagonists.

Fez brought Shelby to his family Thanksgiving, a real event that Ron's comedy then framed through much more provocative romantic/sexual language. The social proximity was real. The exact private meaning should not be reverse-engineered from jokes.

The old Friday Night Lights mechanism had moved inward.

Earlier:

`Fez goes to bar with listeners → Monday radio`

Later:

`Fez brings coworker into family space → radio investigates family space`

The public/private boundary had contracted all the way to the home.

# XIX. Cakehorn: one word can now carry seventeen years

In March 2014 Fez used **“cakehorn”** for someone's mouth.

Whatever the exact origin—and that remains unresolved—Ron immediately recognized that Fez wanted the word to stop being examined.

So he examined it more closely.

What is a cakehorn?

Why horn?

Why cake?

Can it be tooted?

Did Fez mean cakehole?

Every explanation created another branch.

Cakehorn is the purest late-era expression of Ron's method because almost no premise is required.

In the WNEW years, the show could build a Comedy Pyramid and invite a hundred listeners to contribute.

By 2014, one accidental or semi-accidental word could contain enough relationship history to sustain the same process inside the room.

The formal pyramid was gone.

The people had become the pyramid.

## Was the first Cakehorn deliberate?

We do not know.

Competing explanations include verbal mistake, handwriting confusion, “cakehole” confusion, prearranged premise and hybrid stories. Some later explanations were themselves delivered in contexts where joking remained possible.

What **is** certain is that once the word existed, the escalation was deliberate improvisation.

That distinction is the method of this archive in miniature:

**uncertain origin does not mean uncertain everything.**

# XX. Steakgate: when a fake premise finds a real nerve

Steakgate began with one of the stupidest possible arguments.

Fez had promised steaks and produced Steak 'n Shake steakburgers.

Was a steakburger a steak?

The premise appears, based on later retrospective accounts, to have been constructed at least in substantial part.

Then the argument stopped being about hamburgers.

Chris Stanley became unusually angry. The discussion exposed years of frustration over Fez's participation in a show bearing his name.

The current forensic shorthand is:

**WORK → SHOOT → WORK**

A comic premise created the conditions for real conflict. Once the real conflict became uncomfortable enough, the ridiculous premise also gave everybody a route back into performance.

This is a much better model for R&F than asking whether Steakgate was “fake.”

The premise can be worked and the emotion real.

The emotion can then be performed after it becomes real.

That is the show.

# XXI. Failure becomes the format

Late *Ron & Fez* made a discovery few shows would willingly test:

**failure can be better than success.**

If Fez's cat Skippy perfectly demonstrated the behaviors Fez claimed, the segment would end.

When Skippy failed, Ron could ask why.

If Fez's sentimental matchbook story were obviously true, the story would end.

When details became questionable, the investigation could begin.

If an intern handled pressure professionally, there was less radio.

If an employee's explanation made sense, Ron had nowhere to go.

The late-show engine can be written almost mechanically:

`claim → failure → Ron notices → explanation → new contradiction → deeper failure → comedy`

This pattern had always existed with Billy and Earl.

By the end, it could operate on Fez himself.

---

# XXII. Why Ron did not simply replace Fez

By 2014 this question had followed the show for years.

Why keep a co-host who sometimes barely spoke?

The answer becomes easier to understand if “co-host” is the wrong category.

Ron and Fez had worked together since the Florida era. Fez was embedded in the Bennington family. At retirement he spoke of Ron not merely as a colleague but as friend, partner, brother and the person who had made his career possible.

Ron could replace a producer.

He could continue after Dave.

He could start a new show.

He could not replace Fez **and still call the result *Ron & Fez* in any meaningful sense**.

That distinction became concrete in October 2014 when Ron began ***Bennington*** with his daughter Gail while *Ron & Fez* still existed.

Ron no longer faced a binary choice between keeping Fez or ending his own career.

He could let the friendship and the show reach their end on different terms.

---

# XXIII. April Fool's Day, 2015: the show is trapped by its own mythology

On **April 1, 2015**, Fez's retirement was announced.

Many listeners did not believe it.

Of course they did not.

They had spent years learning that staff exits could be storylines, arguments could be partly worked, April Fool's Day explanations could contain another joke, and the sentence “this is not a bit” did not necessarily clarify anything.

Even former staff could initially suspect a prank.

This is the perfect final consequence of the show's epistemology:

The most important factual statement the program could make—**the show is ending**—arrived on the calendar's traditional lying day to an audience professionally trained not to trust appearances.

It was real.

The final live show aired **April 3, 2015**.

Listeners, staff, comedians and old figures said goodbye. Big ASS numbers resurfaced. People described the show less as a collection of favorite bits than as something that had occupied commutes, jobs, relationships and whole periods of adulthood.

**“Satellite of Love”** carried the emotional weight of the ending.

A retrospective week followed. On April 10, Ron gave the final sign-off and *Bennington* moved forward.

The show did not find a new Fez.

It became a different show.

---

# XXIV. Retirement: Fez can finally be a listener

Fez retired to Florida.

One of the most revealing post-show developments is that he appears to have remained a committed listener to *Bennington* and supportive of Gail as Ron's co-host.

For years, the possibility of somebody occupying his place had been threatening.

Once the obligation to occupy the place was gone, the threat could disappear too.

He could be Ron's friend again without needing to prove he was still Ron's functioning radio partner.

In 2018 he made a surprise appearance at a *Bennington* Thanksgiving show at the Hard Rock Cafe in Times Square—a location saturated with R&F history.

It was not the beginning of a comeback.

It was a return to the people.

---

# XXV. August 2021: the old neighborhood comes home

Fez died in August 2021 at the age of 57 after years of serious cardiac problems.

The reaction immediately revealed what remained of the R&F community.

Old staff, comedians, callers, board people, WNEW listeners, Fairfax listeners, XM listeners and people who had discovered the show later through archives all reappeared.

Old vocabulary returned almost instantly.

Budday.

Cakehorn.

Big ASS numbers.

Old characters.

Old songs.

Old arguments.

The memorial *Bennington* broadcast functioned almost like a temporary reconstruction of a live listening thread. When “Satellite of Love” closed the show, the song had accumulated so much shared meaning that it no longer needed explanation.

This was the community in its third major role.

During the active show it had helped **produce** material.

After 2015 it helped **preserve** material.

After Fez's death it became a **memorial network**.

---

# XXVI. FezCon and the museum

On **January 19, 2023**, FezCon brought old personalities and fans together at the Hard Rock Cafe in Times Square.

The logic was nearly identical to the earliest RFNet meetups.

People knew one another by screen names, bits, Big ASS numbers and shared language. Some had interacted for years without having occupied the same physical room.

The internet became bodies again.

Around the same period, **RonFez.net returned as the Fez Marie Whatley Memorial RonFez.Net Media Archives**.

The transformation is almost too neat:

### Then
RFNet was where the community lived.

### Now
RFNet is a museum documenting where the community lived.

Old photographs now do double duty. They show the R&F world, but they also show the younger lives of the people who built it—friends, spouses, apartments, jobs, bars and people who have since died.

The archive preserves a radio show and a social generation at the same time.

---

# XXVII. The oral history is still changing

The record remains alive because participants are still speaking.

East Side Dave's ***Tales From the Satellite*** has become a major source for the XM period.

Al Dukes has participated in WNEW retrospectives.

Tasteless Ginny has resurfaced early photographs and a June 2000 cassette lead.

Don the Hypnotist has publicly revisited his history and even corrected his own memory after finding old files, moving his earliest R&F appearances back from what he remembered as 2007 to evidence showing 2004.

B.L./Brenda Lee continues to speak publicly about the older Florida world.

This is why participant memory receives respect but not automatic supremacy in the archive.

A person is uniquely authoritative about their own experience.

They can still misremember a date twenty years later.

The documentary record and oral history have to correct each other.

---

# XXVIII. The missing archive is part of the story too

Some of the most tantalizing pieces of early RFNet history survive only as titles:

- **Slumber Party Pics**
- **A Night at Double D's**
- **The Group Photo**
- **RonFez.Net on WWF Smackdown!**
- **CyberSoldier Holiday Party Pics 2002**
- **Moshin's Tattoo**

These were not obscure files hidden at the bottom of the site. In the surviving restored library, they rank among the most accessed community artifacts.

That tells us something even before we recover their contents.

RFNet users valued **documentation of themselves**.

Photographs of parties, tattoos, television appearances and group gatherings competed in prominence with famous audio.

The community was preserving its own civilization.

Current technical research has recovered parts of the old RFNet address system, including historical `displaymedia.cfm/id/...` links copied onto other forums and restored numeric `downloads.php?do=file&id=...` records. The exact mapping between the old and restored IDs remains unresolved.

The archive problem is increasingly clear:

**some history may be hidden behind a broken index rather than truly gone.**

---

# XXIX. So what was *Ron & Fez*?

A conventional answer is easy.

It was a comedy radio show hosted by Ron Bennington and Fez Whatley that ran in various forms from the late 1990s until 2015.

That is correct and inadequate.

A fuller answer is:

> ***Ron & Fez* was a long-running improvisational social world in which real people, performed identities, staff relationships, callers, friends, fans and internet communities continuously converted one another's lives into serialized entertainment.**

Ron supplied the framing intelligence: the ability to notice the contradiction, remember the history and keep asking.

Fez supplied a relationship deep enough to remain inexhaustible, and a personality in which performance and private life could never be fully separated.

The staff supplied new human systems for Ron to work on.

The civilians supplied density.

The boards supplied memory and conflict.

Paltalk supplied simultaneity.

The events supplied bodies.

The musicians supplied a second language.

The archivists supplied survival.

And the audience eventually inherited responsibility for the whole thing.

---

# XXX. The most important correction to the popular memory

Late Fez is so psychologically striking that he can erase early Fez in retrospect.

That should not happen.

The story is not:

**Ron the genius carries a broken co-host for years.**

The earlier record contains a fast, funny, aggressive, performative Fez who did editorials, impressions, characters, socialized directly with listeners, held court at gatherings, generated his own comic energy and helped create the relationship chemistry that made the show possible in the first place.

The late years are moving precisely because that earlier person existed.

A smoother, more conventional co-host might have produced an easier workplace.

It would not necessarily have produced *Ron & Fez*.

The thing Ron kept investigating for seventeen years was not merely a comedy premise.

It was **Fez**.

And one of the quieter truths preserved in later memory is that Ron sometimes seemed to perform for an audience of one: when Fez broke into helpless laughter, Ron would keep pushing the line because the person he most wanted to make laugh was sitting a few feet away.

That relationship is why the ending could not be solved by replacement.

It is also why, years after the microphones stopped, the audience kept trying to put the world back together.
