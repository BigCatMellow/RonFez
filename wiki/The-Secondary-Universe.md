# The Secondary Universe

The fan ecosystem around *Ron & Fez* was not commentary attached to the show.

It became one of the systems **from which the show was made**.

At different stages it functioned as:

- social network;
- town square;
- writers' room;
- rumor mill;
- photo archive;
- audio archive;
- event calendar;
- status hierarchy;
- character pipeline;
- live audience room;
- unofficial broadcast-distribution system;
- creator network;
- mutual-aid network;
- memorial community;
- historical preservation system.

The core relationship was bidirectional:

`SHOW ↔ COMMUNITY`

That is the missing piece behind much of R&F's famously dense continuity.

---

# RonFez.net: the town square

The strongest current evidence places RonFez.net's institutional launch around **February 5, 2001**. A 2011 tenth-anniversary post by JustJon directly points back to that date, although some founding-era memories loosely reach into 2000/01.

The board quickly became more than a place to review that day's radio.

Surviving threads show people discussing ordinary life, entertainment, sports, relationships, technology, politics and one another. That means RFNet's defining social transition happened early:

### Stage 1
`I listen to Ron & Fez.`

### Stage 2
`I talk online about Ron & Fez.`

### Stage 3
`I know people because we all listen to Ron & Fez.`

### Stage 4
`I now use RonFez.net to talk to those people about everything.`

At Stage 4, the website is no longer merely a fan forum. It is a social environment.

## Scale

Surviving mature-site statistics report approximately:

- **38,387 members**;
- **84,226 threads**;
- **3,043,602 posts**.

The gallery separately preserves thousands of photographs.

Those totals accumulated over years and should not be mistaken for simultaneous active users, but they establish a large, durable social system.

## Handles become biographies

Long-running accounts accumulated tens of thousands of posts.

A handle could therefore encode:

- years of opinions;
- friends;
- enemies;
- event attendance;
- radio calls;
- relationships;
- photos;
- songs;
- inside jokes;
- status;
- reputation.

When Ron or Dave said the name of a known poster, longtime listeners did not hear a username. They heard the accumulated biography attached to it.

This is one reason old clips are hostile to newcomers: the show can invoke years of social context with one word.

# Who ran RFNet?

The earliest governance history is still being reconstructed, but several roles are now much clearer.

## FoundryMusicJeff / Jeff Shain

A contemporary October 2002 post places Jeff directly in the site's governing and technical layer. He described how he ran RonFez.Net, called it his board, and referred to software used in the adjacent O&A web ecosystem as **his board software**.

This is strong evidence that Jeff was a principal early operator with technical authority.

It does **not** prove he was the sole launch-day founder or legal owner.

## JustJon

Registered essentially at the beginning. Directly visible as an early media uploader and later front-page/event organizer. His exact original formal title remains unresolved, but his institutional continuity is clear.

## Mikeyboy

Publicly self-identifies later as a **former admin**. More importantly for today's history, enormous amounts of restored event photography appear under his account, sometimes with descriptions showing that other people took the original photographs.

He therefore appears to have acted partly as **media custodian/curator**, not merely personal photographer.

## Other governance

Surviving pages directly identify conventional moderators such as `thepaulo`/C_T-era moderator accounts. Other names—MojoPin, early staff-room participants, technical administrators—remain open research questions.

# The board had a culture before Board Gossip

One of the most useful surviving documents is an October 2002 discussion on another contemporary board.

An RFNet user identifies herself as coming from the **“shiney happy ronfez.net board.”** Other posters characterize RFNet as more “candy coated.” The discussion refers to warning emails and contentious posts being moved into a staff area.

Several conclusions follow:

- RFNet had visible moderation by 2002;
- users carried board identities to other sites;
- different boards already had cultural stereotypes;
- cross-board friction existed years before Dave created Board Gossip.

East Side Dave did not invent the underlying sociology.

He gave it a theme song.

# Big ASS: ritual membership

The **Big ASS Card / All Secret Society** gave listeners a deliberately silly formal identity.

Membership numbers could be used when calling and sometimes had contest/sponsor functions, but the social purpose was more durable.

A Big ASS number meant:

**I belong to this world.**

Numbers remained part of listener identity through the 2015 farewell, Fez memorial period and later reunions.

The card linked three selves:

`radio listener ↔ internet identity ↔ physical community member`

# The archive begins almost immediately

RFNet's surviving media history shows that fans were not merely writing posts.

They were creating and preserving:

- soundboards;
- edited audio;
- photographs;
- fan songs;
- event media;
- show clips;
- historically important full broadcasts.

Early soundboards survive from 2001.

The site explicitly preserved the complete **September 11, 2001** show even though full-show distribution was not its normal practice.

That is evidence of historical consciousness unusually early in the community's life.

# Physicalization: usernames become people

The early board community moved offline quickly.

Surviving or reconstructed examples include:

- New York Forever bar crawls;
- the first and second Big ASS Nights of Fights;
- celebrity softball;
- the first RFNet Christmas party in 2002;
- later Fairfax tournaments and gatherings;
- Friday Night Lights;
- XM-era bar nights;
- weddings;
- BBQs;
- RF softball;
- holiday parties;
- memorial events.

Once two posters had met physically, the next online argument was no longer merely anonymous internet conflict.

It could contain real friendship, embarrassment, jealousy, romantic history or an incident both people remembered differently.

That made the board more socially meaningful—and more combustible.

# The first RFNet Christmas party — 2002

This early event is becoming one of the most valuable windows into the founding community.

The restored gallery directly preserves a **Christmas Party 2002** category.

A 2023 discussion around a restored, labeled image identifies it as the **first RF.NET X-Mas party** and says the event was in 2002. Participants remember additional group shots in the archive and recognize a cluster including **HordeKing, Stalker Patti, Hosp and Moshin**.

The venue is remembered as Dave & Buster's at the Palisades mall, plausibly Palisades Center in West Nyack. That remains retrospective evidence pending a contemporary event announcement or broadcast reference.

This party is now a major recovery node because it may connect three otherwise mysterious high-download files:

- `The Group Photo`;
- `CyberSoldier Holiday Party Pics 2002`;
- `Moshin's Tattoo`.

No direct file-ID bridge has yet proven those links.

# Fairfax: the community becomes distribution infrastructure

When R&F moved from New York terrestrial radio to WJFK in Fairfax, many longtime listeners could no longer receive the station conventionally.

The fan community improvised.

Later listeners independently remember:

- **Big ASS Antenna**;
- **Live365**;
- **Radio BBQ**;
- Winamp/internet-radio streams;
- emailed MP3s;
- recordings circulated by **Msteels / Steels**;
- analog methods as crude as feeding computer audio into a tape deck.

This is historically important because the audience was now solving a broadcast problem the official station relationship had not solved for them.

The community had become:

`social network + archive + unofficial distribution system`

# Friday Night Lights: the off-air show

In 2005, Fez regularly gathered with listeners and friends at a Fairfax bar called **Thursdays**.

The recurring institution became known as **Friday Night Lights**.

This was important not because a radio personality went to a bar with fans. Radio personalities do appearances constantly.

The difference was continuity.

Fez was personally involved. The same listeners returned. Ron often was not there. Something could happen Friday, acquire several incompatible versions over the weekend, move through board discussion, and arrive on Monday as a mystery for Ron to investigate.

The fan community therefore acted simultaneously as:

- participants;
- witnesses;
- rumor network;
- evidence pool;
- callers;
- future subjects.

Friday Night Lights may be the purest example of the **off-air show generating the on-air show**.

# Paltalk: simultaneity

RFNet gave listeners persistent history.

Paltalk gave them **live presence**.

The surviving platform page directly establishes:

- room name: **Ron and Fez Big ASS Room**;
- description: **created by the fans for the fans**;
- rule language including `NO BASHING`;
- owner: **French Bread Pizza**;
- hundreds of followers on the surviving listing.

Paltalk could combine chat, persistent names, webcams and live reaction while the radio show was happening.

That changes the feedback loop from asynchronous:

`show → board post → later show reaction`

into near real time:

`show → room reaction → staff sees room → show mentions room → room reacts`

A Paltalk identity could therefore acquire a different form of visibility from a normal board poster or caller.

That matters because there was no single “fan reaction.”

There were rival reactions.

# Board Gossip: the loop becomes a segment

East Side Dave's **Board Gossip** institutionalized the entire system.

The probable launch sequence is:

- **November 6, 2006** — later compilation labels a failed/partial start;
- **November 8, 2006** — compilation labels full debut;
- by March 2007 — contemporary outside fans are already quoting the segment's presentation;
- by May 2007 — official/publisher metadata lists Board Gossip as established programming.

The full November tapes survive and still need exact primary indexing before the launch labels become final A-level chronology.

The segment's basic machinery was self-renewing:

1. R&F airs something.
2. Fans post about it.
3. Fans fight with one another or staff.
4. Dave reads the boards.
5. Dave reports the gossip.
6. Ron reacts to Dave's report.
7. The people discussed react online.
8. Dave can report that reaction next time.

The audience became both **reviewer and source material**.

# Status, proximity and the civilian hierarchy

The mature community developed an informal social economy.

A rough progression could look like:

1. listener;
2. caller;
3. registered poster;
4. recognizable poster;
5. Paltalk regular;
6. event attendee;
7. person known to prominent fans;
8. person known to staff;
9. person who socializes with show figures;
10. studio visitor;
11. recurring radio subject;
12. “friend of the show.”

These were not official ranks.

They still mattered.

Recognition had status value. That could create friendship, creative opportunity and belonging. It could also create cliques, resentment and behavior intended to earn attention.

The show often mocked people for caring too much about board status.

But when Ron and Dave discussed those people on national satellite radio, the show made the status more valuable.

This is one of the central paradoxes of the secondary universe:

> **R&F ridiculed the hierarchy while constantly reinforcing it.**

# Fan creation becomes production

The community generated actual usable media.

## Songs
Perrynoid, Sleeves and other musicians converted show incidents into reusable audio.

## Soundboards
Early RFNet soundboards demonstrate fan remixing of the show's sonic language months after the site's launch.

## Photography
Mikeyboy, Mike the Teacher, Fallon and others preserved the physical cast and events.

## Episode memory
Fans maintained dates, logs, threads and narratives that later researchers still rely on.

## Audio preservation
Collectors preserved clips and, in special cases, full shows.

## Independent programs
By 2007, Ron's **Night of a Hundred Podcasts** concept was remembered as giving friends-of-show independent media actual SiriusXM distribution.

The audience-to-creator pipeline had become literal programming.

# A daughter community: The Hideout / Heretics

Fairfax-era participant J-Dubs / Justin Johnson later co-hosted ***The Hideout***.

The surviving Hideout archive describes a fan population called **The Heretics** that developed its own boards, online fights, media and show interaction.

This suggests a remarkable cultural transmission:

`R&F participatory radio environment → participant becomes broadcaster → new show develops its own participatory fan environment`

The secondary universe could reproduce itself.

# Mutual aid and community beyond comedy

The social network sometimes had practical functions.

The Hurricane Isabel broadcast temporarily turned callers into local information sources.

RFNet funeral and memorial appeals—such as material surrounding **Irish Alkey**—show the community coordinating support around death and family need.

Gvac's memorial demonstrates relationships lasting many years beyond ordinary fandom.

After Fez's death, listeners described friendships, marriages and family relationships that began through the show.

This is why describing the whole phenomenon as merely **parasocial** is incomplete.

There was certainly a parasocial axis:

`listener → broadcaster`

But there was also extensive genuine social life:

`listener ↔ listener`

The two reinforced each other.

# RFNet fragmentation and v3

The original RFNet did not simply “die one day.”

The surviving evidence points toward a long fragmentation process involving:

- accumulated personal conflict;
- shrinking listening threads;
- rival communities;
- migration to FBA and other boards;
- a later remembered **Most Post Wins** episode;
- a directly evidenced offshoot at **ronfezv3.com**;
- migration toward Twitter/Facebook/YouTube/social media;
- Dave's departure removing the show's most formal board-to-radio bridge;
- the show's own shift toward a smaller studio-centered cast.

Original RFNet posts from August 2010 directly reference `v3` and even embed a `ronfezv3.com` asset, so v3 is period fact rather than later folklore.

What remains unproven is a monocausal story such as **“Chuckwagon killed RFNet.”** Later users blame Most Post Wins and specific personalities to varying degrees, but broader structural fragmentation was already happening.

# The community changes jobs

## Active-show period
**Production system**

It generates callers, characters, feuds, songs, photographs, events and gossip.

## Post-2015
**Preservation system**

It keeps old recordings, photographs, logs and vocabulary alive.

## After Fez's death in 2021
**Memorial system**

Old users return and reconstruct social ties.

## 2023 onward
**Historical system**

FezCon, the restored RFNet archive, Dave's oral history, participant recollections and synchronized listening turn the old audience into historians of its own world.

# RFNet returns as museum

The restored **Fez Marie Whatley Memorial RonFez.Net Media Archives** did not simply recreate the old interactive board.

Its symbolic function changed.

### Original RFNet
**Where the community lived.**

### Restored RFNet
**A museum showing where the community lived.**

This is why the archive matters beyond radio history.

For longtime users, old photos can preserve their own adulthood: friendships, old relationships, apartments, bars, jobs, people who died and versions of themselves that no longer exist.

The archive is simultaneously a history of *Ron & Fez* and a history of the people who formed around it.

# The current recovery problem

Several early artifacts survive only through titles and popularity counters because the old RFNet media index is broken or only partially restored.

Historical external pages preserve old item URLs in the form:

`ronfez.net/displaymedia.cfm/id/<number>`

The restored library uses:

`downloads.php?do=file&id=<number>`

It is not yet established whether those ID systems map directly.

The current forensic recovery strategy is therefore:

1. find copied historical deep links on outside forums/blogs;
2. recover old numeric IDs;
3. compare them against restored pages;
4. use gallery category/filename/comment metadata as a parallel reconstruction path;
5. use Wayback/Common Crawl URL indexes when technically accessible;
6. connect media records back to contemporaneous threads and audio.

# The central conclusion

The secondary universe was not a particularly intense fan club.

It was a **bidirectional media/social system**.

The show created the community.

The community created stories, people, media and conflicts.

Those returned to the show.

The show transformed them.

The community preserved the transformation.

And after the show ended, that same community became the reason we can reconstruct so much of it at all.
